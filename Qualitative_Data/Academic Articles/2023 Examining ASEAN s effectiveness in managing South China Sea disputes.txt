ABSTRACT

For the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN), the South China Sea
(SCS) disputes between China and Southeast Asian claimant states (Vietnam,
the Philippines, Malaysia, and Brunei) are a major concern. However, the dominant sceptics are pessimistic about ASEAN’s effectiveness in managing these
disputes. This article contributes to the field by providing a systematic analysis
on this topic, which is missing within the literature. Also, many of the arguments presented in this article challenge the dominant views and thus provide
an alternative understanding of ASEAN’s effectiveness in managing the disputes. The arguments are as follows: First, ASEAN members do have shared
interests and a common policy regarding managing the disputes. Second,
China’s influence on individual ASEAN members is not as strong as the sceptics
often argue. Third, the degree of ASEAN’s goal attainment is medium because
it achieved essential goals while conceding important but non-essential ones.
Fourth, compared with a legalistic and adversarial posturing approach, ASEAN’s
approach is more effective in managing these disputes.

KEYWORDS South China Sea disputes; ASEAN-China relationship; ASEAN’s effectiveness;

China; ASEAN

Introduction
The Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) considers the South
China Sea (SCS) disputes between China and Southeast Asian claimant
states (Vietnam, the Philippines, Malaysia, and Brunei) highly concerning for
multiple reasons. First, the disputes have been a prominent flashpoint in
the Southeast Asian region (Severino, 2014), impacting ASEAN’s claimant
and non-claimant members and undermining regional peace. Second, the
disputes are a stumbling block for deepening ASEAN-China ties. For

organization of Southeast Asian states, ASEAN failed to support its claimant
members’ interests in the SCS (Ba, 2016) or confront China’s illegal SCS
claims (Le Thu, 2019). Second, facing China’s superior economic and military
power, ASEAN is not unified and has been ineffective in managing the disputes or preserving its self-proclaimed leadership and centrality in regional
affairs (Cook, 2018; Yahuda, 2019). Third, ASEAN’s working mechanism,
characterised by consensus decision-making, face-saving, and informality,
has been manipulated by China and lacks effectiveness in managing the
disputes (Beeson, 2020; Jones & Jenne, 2016).
However, despite these criticisms, there is a dearth of systematic analysis
of ASEAN’s effectiveness in managing the SCS disputes. Thus, this article
aims to fill this gap by examining to what extent ASEAN is effective in managing these disputes. Moreover, the article’s findings challenge many arguments made by the sceptics and thus could provide an alternative
evaluation of ASEAN’s effectiveness in the context of SCS disputes.
This article’s main arguments are as follows: First, ASEAN members do
have shared interests and a common policy regarding managing SCS disputes. Second, China’s influence on individual ASEAN members is not as
strong as the sceptics argue. Third, the degree of ASEAN’s external effectiveness (goal attainment) is medium, as it achieved some essential goals
while making compromises on important but non-essential ones. Fourth,
compared with a legalistic and ‘adversarial posturing’ approach (Acharya,
2001, as cited in Eaton & Stubbs, 2006, p.138), ASEAN’s approach is more
effective in managing the disputes.

Analytical framework
While effectiveness is widely viewed as an important criterion for evaluating
a political organisation’s performance, its definition varies, with the most
common being an actor’s ability to achieve its stated goals (Groen, 2019).
This definition has two major components: the actor’s ability and stated
goals. Scholars generally view goal attainment as an important dimension
for measuring an organisations’ effectiveness, but they do not all agree that
its ability to act or ‘actorness’ is another dimension (Elsig, 2013; Groen &
Niemann, 2013; Thomas, 2012). This article thinks that actorness often positively correlates with an organisation’s goal attainment and thus should be
a major dimension for measuring an organization’s effectiveness. Like some
scholars, this article defines an organisation’s goal attainment as ‘external
effectiveness’ and actorness as ‘internal effectiveness’ (Carbone, 2013,
p.343, Van Schaik, 2013, p.38).
Regarding external effectiveness, there are two concerns. First, an actor
may have conflicting goals, which cannot be fully achieved simultaneously.
The actor has to strike a balance between these goals. For instance, regarding SCS disputes, China has conflicting goals; it wishes to consolidate its
nationalist claims while preserving relatively benign relationships with its
neighbouring countries (Fravel, 2017). Thus, China is ‘threading the needle’
(Fravel, 2017, p.223) to strike a balance. Second, to reach an agreement,
negotiating parties often need to make concessions. Thus, only in rare circumstances, can an actor fully achieve its stated goals.
As an actor is often unable to fully achieve its stated goals, it is impractical to examine its external effectiveness in an either-or manner (effective or
not effective). Instead, the degree of external effectiveness should be

measured ‘along a continuum going from low to medium to high effectiveness’ (da Conceiç~ao-Heldt & Meunier, 2014, p.968). In this article, ASEAN’s
external effectiveness is deemed high when it achieves most of its stated
goals and concedes unimportant interests. ASEAN’s external effectiveness is
considered medium when it achieves bottom-line goals, obtains some
important concessions from other relevant parties, and concedes other
important but not bottom-line interests. ASEAN’s external effectiveness is
considered low when it achieves the least and concedes the most.
Regarding internal effectiveness, scholars examining the EU’s internal
effectiveness have proposed many criteria. Bretherton and Vogler (2013, pp.
386–387) examined internal effectiveness through ‘opportunity, presence
and capability’. Groen and Niemann (2013, p.309) analysed internal effectiveness through ‘recognition, authority, coherence, and autonomy’. Brattberg
and Rhinard (2013, p. 359) proposed ‘context, coherence, capability, consistency’. Many criteria in these frameworks overlap, such as coherence and recognition. However, as these scholars mainly analysed the EU’s internal
effectiveness, some of their criteria are not applicable to the ASEAN context.
For instance, authority (Jupille & Caporaso, 1998, p. 216, da Conceiç~ao-Heldt
& Meunier, 2014, p.964) refers ‘to the extent of delegated competences from
the member states to the EU’. Thus, authority is a concept related to sovereignty pooling, meaning that some member states could be outvoted
(McLean & McMillan, 2018). However, ASEAN’s mechanism is consensus decision-making, in which member states cannot be outvoted.
Drawing on the available frameworks, this article chose four criteria that
are helpful in analysing ASEAN’s internal effectiveness in SCS dispute management. The first criterion is coherence. Specifically, this article analyses to
what extent ASEAN members share interests regarding SCS disputes
(‘preference coherence’) and whether ASEAN has a common policy to manage SCS disputes (‘output coherence’) (Groen & Niemann, 2013, p.310).
The second criterion is ‘consistency’ (Brattberg & Rhinard, 2013, p.359),
which refers to whether ASEAN members persist in carrying out ASEAN’s
common policy rather than ignoring it or pursuing their national interests.
The third criterion is ASEAN’s power. Power is defined as the ability to
‘get others to do what they would otherwise not do’ (Nye, 1990, p.154).
Drawing on da Conceiç~ao-Heldt’s (2014) and Groen’s (2019) works, this article first examines ASEAN’s power resources/instruments in SCS disputes.
Second, this article postulates that if ASEAN has more power resources than
China in some dimensions, it will compel China to make concessions.
However, because China has important leverage regarding SCS disputes,
ASEAN will consider China’s position and make some important but not
bottom-line concessions. Third, ASEAN’s contradictory shared interests may
constrain its ability to compel China.

The fourth criterion is recognition (Delreux, 2014), that is, whether
ASEAN is recognised by relevant parties as a legitimate player in managing
the disputes. If so, ASEAN’s ability to attain goals will be enhanced.
Otherwise, ASEAN has to establish recognition as a goal to enhance its ability to act.
In addition to internal and external effectiveness, an important idea
stemming from the literature is that an organisation’s effectiveness should
be compared. Acharya and Johnston (2007, p. 2) analyse regional institutions by examining ‘whether variation in institutional design leads to variation in … efficacy of these institutions for resolving regional cooperation
problems.’ For SCS disputes, one important debate is whether an ASEAN
approach that ‘emphasises consultation, consensus decision-making, informality, and a non-confrontational bargaining style’ (Stubbs, 2019, p. 928) is
more effective in helping ASEAN achieve its goals than a legalistic and
adversarial posturing approach. Scholars analysing the EU’s external effectiveness argue that a political organisation’s external effectiveness not only
depends on its internal effectiveness but also on the context in which it
operates (Groen & Niemann, 2013). The context mainly includes relevant
parties’ positions, which facilitate or constrain the organisation’s actions
(Groen & Niemann, 2013). Because China is the most powerful claimant
state and makes the most extensive claim, the key to comparing the
approaches’ effectiveness is ascertaining which approach is more effective
in pushing China into SCS dispute management and helping ASEAN attain
its goals.
In short, this article analyses ASEAN’s effectiveness in managing SCS disputes by examining its internal effectiveness (coherence, consistency,
power, and recognition) and external effectiveness (goal attainment). It also
compares the ASEAN approach with an adversarial posturing and legalistic
approach by examining which approach is more effective in pushing China
into SCS dispute management and facilitating ASEAN’s goal attainment. As
the extent to which ASEAN members share interests regarding SCS disputes
is the basis for its internal effectiveness and for the goals that ASEAN needs
to achieve, this article examines coherence first.

ASEAN’s internal effectiveness-coherence
Some scholars doubt ASEAN’s capability to protect its member states’ interests in SCS disputes (Ba, 2016; Beeson, 2020). However, this argument lacks
nuance. ASEAN functions effectively when its member states ‘join hands to
advance common interests when and where they deemed fit’ (Acharya,
2016, p.118). Thus, regarding SCS disputes, ASEAN is responsible for protecting member states’ shared interests, but not their distinctive national

interests. As these shared interests are the basis of ASEAN’s common policy
on SCS disputes, it is important to distinguish shared interests from the
national interests of individual member states.
First, ASEAN states prefer to manage SCS disputes through an ASEAN-led
approach. As a grouping of Southeast Asian states built to promote
‘regional peace and stability’ (ASEAN, 1967), ASEAN has interests in managing the disputes that deeply affect regional peace. Moreover, claimant
states are more inclined to seek ASEAN’s help to constrain China’s assertive
actions at sea than to bilaterally negotiate with China (Guan, 1998;
Odgaard, 2003). Non-claimant members also endorse the ASEAN-led
approach. In 1999, Thailand’s then Foreign Minister Surin Pitsuwan (1999,
as cited in Chung, 2009, p.102) said that SCS issues must be discussed at
the ASEAN Foreign Minister Meeting (AMM) because conflicts at sea ‘would
be adverse to all of us’. Cambodia, while opposing the ‘internationalisation
of the disputes’, stated that they must be managed within the ASEANChina framework (Tan, 2012).
Second, ASEAN members have a shared interest in avoiding unduly escalating the SCS disputes and completely breaking down their relationships
with China. The non-claimant and mild claimant ASEAN states’ interests in
this regard have been widely recognised. The two assertive claimant states,
Vietnam and the Philippines, share this interest as well. In October 2019,
when Vietnamese citizens expressed their concern about China’s challenges

Third, ASEAN members share an interest in avoiding embroiling the
region in the US-China power play. Since its founding, ASEAN has aimed to
free the Southeast Asian region from ‘involvement in the quarrels of the
strong’ (Severino, 2006, p.10). While many ASEAN states welcome the US
presence in the region, they desire moderate US-China competition, which
would afford them space to manoeuvre between the great powers and
maximise their autonomy (Han, 2018). The deepening US involvement in
the disputes, guided by the Freedom of Navigation operation or its aim to
prevent China from ‘bullying’ other claimant states (Esper, 2020, as cited in
Lendon, 2020), may trigger Chinese countermeasures or even backlash. In
consequence, the SCS may become a field of US-China competition
(Jaipragas, 2018, Graham, 2020). This would compel regional states to
choose sides.
Some observers may argue that claimant countries may side with the US
because of China’s assertive actions in the SCS. However, this article argues
that although SCS disputes are a major cause of friction between ASEAN
claimants and China, they are not threatening enough for the claimants to
choose sides. A 2012 Vietnam Academy of Social Sciences report argues
that China does not pose a ‘lethal threat’ to Vietnam’s sovereignty and
independence, and it will not threaten Vietnam’s sovereignty beyond the
disputed area (Nguy~^en, 2012, as cited in Zhao, 2020, p.107). The report
views the SCS disputes as a ‘partial dispute’ between the two countries

These shared interests are contradictory. First, using ASEAN forums or
strong wording in forum communiques to address SCS disputes may antagonise China and further escalate tensions. Second, to push China towards

could trigger a backlash from China, thereby intensifying the great power

Although ASEAN states share some interests, defending the claimant
members’ sovereignty claims is not one of them, because only four ASEAN
members are claimants and they dispute each other’s claims. To list a few
examples, in March 2020, Malaysia placed an oil rig in an area of the
extended continental shelf that Vietnam also claims (Vu, 2020). In August
2020, Vietnam protested that the Philippines had given official names to
some land features in the Spratly (VNA, 2020a). From July to August 2020,
Malaysia and the Philippines argued about their respective sovereignty
claims over the Borneo state of Sabah (Regalado, 2020).
Some analysts may argue that ASEAN does not need to defend claimant
members’ sovereignty claims, but that it should defend their legal sovereignty rights, entitled by the 2016 arbitration tribunal awards. However, to
others, this argument is somewhat hypocritical because even ASEAN claimant
members do not fully obey the awards. The tribunal determined that all land
features in the Spratly cannot generate Exclusive Economic Zones (EEZ) (The
Republic of Philippines [PH] v. The People’s Republic of China [PRC], 2016).
This means they are rocks or low tide elevations. If the land features are lowtide elevations, they are not eligible to generate sovereignty and thus belong
to the states whose EEZs they are located within. Vietnam occupies 27 land
features in Spratly. However, although Vietnam has been pressuring China to
obey the award, so far, Vietnam has not conceded its occupied low tide elevations within the Philippine or Malaysian EEZs (Zhao, 2019). Some may
argue that the award is only binding to China and the Philippines. However,
if so, ASEAN has no legal right to enforce the awards as a third party.
Based on the shared interests of its members, ASEAN has formed a common policy to manage SCS disputes. The common policy is Code of
Conduct for the SCS (COC), which is the only dispute management mechanism that allows ASEAN members to reach a consensus. Even Cambodia,
which is recognised as China’s ASEAN ally, has endorsed the COC (Shanghai
Evening Post, 2012).

Thus far, the COC is the most viable mechanism through which ASEAN
members can, to a certain degree, realise their shared interests regarding
SCS disputes. First, this is an ASEAN-driven approach. Thus, it reflects
ASEAN’s centrality and leadership in managing SCS disputes. Second, the
COC is the only binding multilateral SCS dispute management mechanism
that is endorsed by both Southeast Asian claimant states and China. Thus, a
relatively robust and binding COC can restrain the claimants from engaging
in provocative actions, thereby de-escalating tensions at sea and improving
their relationships to a certain degree. Third, by ameliorating frictions, the
COC may reduce ASEAN claimants’ need to involve the US in the disputes.
This will alleviate China’s fear that it will be besieged by a US-led coalition
and need to assertively respond, thereby decreasing the intensity of the
great power competition.
Overall, regarding SCS disputes, ASEAN members favour an ASEANdriven approach. They also share interests in avoiding unduly escalating
tension, completely breaking down their relationships with China, and letting the disputes embroil Southeast Asia in the great power competition.
However, ASEAN members do not share interests in defending claimant
states’ sovereignty claims or claimed legal rights. Based on the shared interests, ASEAN forms a common policy of concluding the COC. However, as
the shared interests are contradictory, they may have a negative impact on
ASEAN’s consistency in realising the interests and the COC.

ASEAN’s internal effectiveness-consistency
Many sceptics argue that ASEAN lacks consistency when managing SCS disputes. Le Thu (2019) argues that ASEAN is incapable of creating a coherent
response to China’s assertive actions in the SCS because China has successfully exploited ASEAN members’ divergent threat-benefit calculations
through a divide and rule strategy. Likewise, Yahuda (2019, p.42) argues
that ‘the ASEAN way that has prized consensus … is turning out to be well
suited to manipulation by China’. The most cited evidence of this argument
is that under China’s pressure, Cambodia vetoed Vietnam and the
Philippines’ proposal which had specified disputed SCS areas in the 2012
AMM’s joint communique (Gill & Schreer, 2018). This led to ASEAN’s failure
to issue a communique for the first time since its founding, revealing its
lack of unity. The second solid piece of evidence is that at the 2016 special
Kunming Meeting between China and ASEAN, China heavy-handedly
blocked ASEAN’s statement (Thayer, 2016a).
This article argues that while China does decrease ASEAN’s consistency
in managing SCS disputes, its influence is not as significant as the sceptics argue.

all ASEAN members endorse an ASEAN-led approach but using ASEAN-led

Also, Malaysia and Indonesia proposed the less confrontational phrase
‘disputed area’, which Cambodia went on to endorse (Thayer, 2012). This

However, the Philippines and Vietnam rejected this proposal and used
another ASEAN interest to support their argument. They insisted that
ASEAN-led forums must stress the tension in the specific area (Thayer,
2012). Thus, examining the meeting in detail did not show that ASEAN was
divided into two, with Cambodia representing China’s interests and the
other ASEAN members wishing to address concerns by specifying the area
of tension. In addition to the China factor, ASEAN’s disunity occurred
because of its members’ insistence on realising ASEAN’s contradictory interests. Hayton (2014) indicated that most ASEAN countries stressed the
importance of ASEAN’s unity and that Cambodia’s rejection caused the disunity. However, ASEAN’s unity could also have been achieved if Vietnam
and the Philippines had accepted the wording of ‘disputed area’ proposed
by Malaysia and Indonesia and endorsed by Cambodia.
Moreover, what often goes unnoticed is ASEAN members’ consistency in
proceeding with drafting the COC and pushing China into the COC talks
since 2012 despite China’s reluctance. From 2011 to early 2013, China was
very reluctant to endorse COC talks (Zhang, 2018), as evidenced by the
then Chinese Foreign Minister Yang Jiechi’s statement in 2011 that ‘China
would be willing to negotiate COC with ASEAN when conditions were ripe’
(People’s Net, 2011). However, despite China’s unwillingness, in May 2012,
as the ASEAN chair, Cambodia announced that ASEAN had concluded key
elements of the COC draft and would commence negotiations with China
(Vong, 2012). Also, at the 2012 AMM meeting, ASEAN members generally

members spoke with one voice to pressure China to start COC negotiations

The 2016 Kunming meeting also demonstrates that China’s influence on
some ASEAN members is not as significant as the sceptics argue. Chinese
Foreign Minister Wang Yi did replace ASEAN’s statement with China’s 10point consensus at the meeting on June 14 (Thayer, 2016a). China also
asked Laos and Cambodia to block the statement (Thayer, 2016a). As a
result, ASEAN failed to issue the statement (Thayer, 2016a). Thus, this is a
solid example that China blocked ASEAN’s statement by asking some
ASEAN members for a favour.
However, this incident also demonstrated ASEAN’s unity in the face of
Chinese pressure (Singh et al., 2016). To abide by ASEAN’s decision, the
organization’s coordinator for the ASEAN-China dialogue, Balakrishnan did
not present at the press conference held for the special meeting and let
Wang present by himself (Singh et al., 2016). While ASEAN explained that it
did not want to publicly disagree with Wang (Balakrishnan, 2016, as cited in
Thayer, 2016a), making Wang attend the conference alone was a signal of
ASEAN’s anger. This is because ASEAN’s decision contradicted its norm of
saving face. Also, it seems that China failed to persuade its ASEAN friends
to attend the conference to show that China was not isolated. This is
another demonstration of ASEAN’s unity.
Finally, although Cambodia and Laos backtracked on their endorsement
of the joint statement (Thayer, 2016a), they had revealed dissatisfaction and
demonstrated ASEAN’s unity against China’s pressure before the meeting.

not issue a statement and denied reaching any agreement with China
(Singh et al., 2016). These reactions reveal that the countries indirectly protested against China’s heavy-handedness and attached more importance to
ASEAN’s common position (Singh et al., 2016).
Overall, by examining these two oft-cited examples regarding ASEAN’s
consistency, the article argues that China is not as effective in manipulating
ASEAN’s consensus decision-making mechanism as previously argued by
sceptics. First, China’s pressure on Cambodia was not the only cause of
ASEAN’s failure to issue the 2012 AMM communique, because ASEAN members’ shared but contradictory interests factored in as well. Also, from 2012
to 2013, China failed to prevent unified ASEAN members from proceeding
with drafting the COC and compelling China into COC talks. Finally, during
the 2016 Kunming special meeting between ASEAN and China, Chinese
Foreign Minister Wang Yi successfully prevented ASEAN from collectively
issuing a statement. However, his action caused unified opposition from
ASEAN. Also, Wang failed to obtain endorsements from China’s ASEAN
friends regarding the four-point consensus before the meeting.

ASEAN’s internal effectiveness—power, recognition, and
external effectiveness
Within this section, this article examines ASEAN’s power, recognition, and
external effectiveness (goal attainment) altogether, because they are interrelated. ASEAN’s ability to compel China to accept the organization’s preferences by utilising its power resources/instruments is vital for its external
effectiveness. Also, if ASEAN is recognised as a legitimate party in managing
SCS disputes, the organization will find it easier to achieve goals. Otherwise,
recognition becomes the goal to enhance its ability to act.
Regarding ASEAN’s instruments and external effectiveness, scholars generally argue that the US role as an external balancer can help ASEAN counter China’s advantageous power and compel the country to accept
undesired outcomes. Jones and Jenne (2016) argue that in the 1990s,
China’s heavy-handed SCS policy increased the possibility of the US’ deeper
involvement in the disputes, which compelled China to adopt a softer
approach in the 2000s. Likewise, Murphy (2017) argues that in the 1990s,
the US role as the balancer successfully softened China’s assertive SCS
actions, and the Philippines and Vietnam have continued seeking the US
assistance to constrain China since 2010. Ge (2012) argues that ASEAN’s
strategy of using great powers, such as the US, to balance against China
has facilitated ‘ASEANisation’ of the disputes.
However, many sceptics criticise the ASEAN way for its ineffectiveness in
addressing SCS disputes. The common criticism of the ASEAN way is that

THE PACIFIC REVIEW

131

ASEAN-led institutions are talk shops (Emmerson, 2017). Jones and Smith
(2007, pp.175-177) argue that the ASEAN way ‘makes process not progress’
in changing China’s preferences and serves China’s self-interest rather than
addressing the disputes. Likewise, Beeson (2018, p. 144) argues that China’s
actions in the SCS ‘gives little cause for optimism about the capacity of
ASEAN-inspired institutions to make a decisive difference to great
power behavior’.
In contrast to the sceptics outside China, Chinese scholars generally
argue that the ASEAN way has successfully pressured China to accept
undesired outcomes. Pang (2012) argues that China has been caught in
ASEAN’s ‘diplomatic trap’, compelled to recognise the norm of not using
force to recover unoccupied land features, and increasingly constrained by
ASEAN-led multilateralism. Nie (2013) argues that ASEAN’s non-confrontational, face-saving, and gradualist approach reduced China’s distrust and
cautiously led China into ASEAN’s desired multilateral approach of handling
SCS disputes. Lu (2016) argues that since 2012, ASEAN has been increasingly proactive and uncompromising in dealing with SCS disputes, ignoring
China’s preference for a bilateral approach and harming the ChinaASEAN friendship.
This article joins this debate by examining to what extent ASEAN could
compel China into COC negotiation and attain the organization’s stated
goals concerning COC, ASEAN’s common policy of handling SCS disputes.
Specifically, this article analyses ASEAN’s instruments for pressuring China
and to what extent ASEAN could achieve its goals by using these
instruments.
To begin, in 2011, amidst the rising SCS tension, ASEAN began to officially push to conclude the COC. At the July AMM, Indonesian President
Yudhoyono stressed the importance of the COC conclusion (BBC News,
2011). Also, during the November EAS, the Indonesian Foreign Minister
Marty Natalegawa said that the ASEAN Foreign Ministers had discussed the
feasibility of pushing for a legally binding COC negotiation and including
China in the negotiations as soon as possible (Zhang & Ji, 2011). In terms of
his statement, ASEAN discussed two COC goals that are legally binding and
early conclusion.
However, ASEAN faced a recognition problem, because from 2011 to
early 2013, China was reluctant to recognise the necessity and legitimacy of
completing the COC. According to an article published by People’s
Daily, China thought that some ASEAN claimants overtly relied on ASEAN’s
multilateral forums to pressure China to accept undesired terms
(Zhongsheng, 2011). According to China, these states did not aim to create
peace but to ‘constrain China and loosen their hands and feet’ through the
COC (Zhongsheng, 2011).

132

L. HU

Moreover, China was reluctant to recognise ASEAN’s legitimacy for pushing the COC conclusion. ASEAN did have the legitimacy because all ASEAN
states and China had signed the Declaration on Conduct of Parties in the
SCS (DOC), which prescribes that all signatories ‘agree to work … towards
the eventual attainment of’ the COC (ASEAN & China, 2002). However,
China was dissatisfied that to increase ASEAN’s internal unity and international influence, ASEAN pushed for the COC conclusion and internationalised the disputes (Zhongsheng, 2011). In 2011, a People’s Daily report stated
that the COC talks were delayed because ‘some states wanted ASEAN to
negotiate the COC with China as a group’ (People’s Net & People’s Daily,
2011). This hinted at China’s insistence on bilateral talks regarding SCS dispute management.
This article will not judge China’s view. However, because of this view,
China continued declining ASEAN’s request to begin the COC talks from
2011 to 2013 (Nie, 2013). However, as noted in the last section, ASEAN persisted in pressuring China to start the COC negotiation from 2012 to 2013.
Also, from 2013 to 2016, ASEAN’s joint communiques for the AMMs were
increasingly hardline regarding the SCS disputes despite China’s opposition
(Lu, 2016). First, SCS disputes were often referred to in the communiques
(Lu, 2016). For instance, in the 2013, 2014, 2015, and 2016 communiques,
two (points 90-91), six (points 150-155), seven (points 150-156), and five
(points 174-178) points referred to SCS disputes respectively (Lu, 2016).
Second, the wording became increasingly strong in these communiques
(Lu, 2016; Storey, 2018). For instance, while the 2013 communique was generally positive (ASEAN, 2013), the 2014 communique expressed a ‘serious
concern over recent developments which had increased tension in the SCS’
(ASEAN, 2014). This hinted at China’s deployment of an oil rig in the waters
that China claimed to be an overlapping area between the Paracel Islands
and Vietnam’s EEZs but over which Vietnam claimed exclusive EEZ rights.
Compared to the 2014 joint communique, the 2015 version was more specific in noting the events that caused regional tensions. It expressed ‘serious
concerns … on the land reclamations in the SCS, which … may undermine
peace … in the SCS’ (ASEAN, 2015). The 2016 communique also noted a
‘serious concern’ over land reclamations in the SCS (ASEAN, 2016).
In addition to ASEAN’s strong diplomatic pressure, ASEAN or ASEAN
members’ cooperation with the US put pressure on China. According to an
article written by the Chinese National People’s Congress’s Deputy Director
of Foreign Affair Committee, Fu Ying, and the President of Chinese National
Institute for SCS Studies, Wu Shicun, since 2010, the US has used ‘political,
diplomatic … and military instruments to increase China’s operational cost
in the SCS, compel China to concede, and contain so-called Chinese expansion in the SCS’ (Fu & Wu, 2016). From China’s perspective, one of the

THE PACIFIC REVIEW

133

political and diplomatic instruments was ASEAN-led multilateral forums,
which facilitated US ‘interference’ in the disputes and its diplomatic support
to the ASEAN claimants (Zhongsheng, 2011). For instance, some ASEAN
states claimed that they would cooperate with the US to solve SCS disputes
at the 2011 AMM (Zhongsheng, 2011). Regarding the military instruments,
the US has increased military presence in the SCS and military support to
the ASEAN claimants since 2011 (Fu & Wu, 2016). For instance, since 2011,
the US warships have continued patrolling near or within the territorial
waters of the China-controlled Paracel Islands and Spratly land features (Fu
& Wu, 2016). Also, since 2011, the US has increased intelligence cooperation
and military drills with Vietnam and the Philippines (Fu & Wu, 2016).
ASEAN’s diplomatic pressure and cooperation with the US has proved to
be fruitful, because since 2013, China conceded to the ASEAN-driven multilateral approach. In early 2013, China signalled a willingness to start the
COC talks (Thayer, 2017). In July 2013, China formally accepted Brunei’s suggestion of a ‘two-track approach’, wherein claimants should directly solve
sovereign and maritime disputes, and ‘China and ASEAN countries should
work together to maintain peace … in the Sea’ (Xinhua, 2016). China formally consulted with ASEAN regarding the COC in September 2013 (Thayer,
2017). This shows that despite its reluctance, China officially recognised
ASEAN as a legitimate party for managing the disputes and had to begin
the COC negotiation. As bilateral talks aimed at solving sovereignty disputes between China and claimant countries are unlikely to be realised in
the foreseeable future, China to a certain degree conceded ASEAN’s major
demand to endorse the ASEAN-led multilateral approach for managing
SCS disputes.
Moreover, because China was unable to prevent ASEAN from being
increasingly hardline and compelling the country to accept the talks, China
probably considered that it had to concede and join the talks to alleviate
ASEAN’s pressure and shape the COC talks. First, in contrast to ASEAN’s
preference for an early conclusion to the COC, China said that the COC
negotiations should not be rushed (Xinhua, 2013). Second, China prefers a
COC that is not legally binding (Storey, 2017). Third, China states that
‘China and ASEAN members should overcome disturbances’ (Xinhua, 2013).
This indicates that China wanted to use the negotiations to constrain
ASEAN’s attempts to involve the US in the disputes.
Regarding SCS disputes, China is the most powerful disputant and makes
the most maritime claims. Thus, China’s approval is a must if any dispute
management mechanism is expected to be effective. This gives China leverage to compel ASEAN to concede in the COC talks. First, contrary to
ASEAN’s preference for an early conclusion to the COC, the COC talks progressed slowly from September 2013 to July 2016. Some observers argued

134

L. HU

that this was because China ‘dragged [its] feet’ (Storey, 2016, p.143; Panda,
2015). Second, ASEAN conceded to China’s demand for a non-legally binding COC. In the ASEAN-China COC framework that was concluded in 2017,
ASEAN omitted the legally binding phrase (Storey, 2017).
Third, although with the help of the US, ASEAN could to some degree
balance against China’s hard power, over-reliance on US support could lead
to a backlash from China and thus embroil the region in a great power
competition. Moreover, embroiling the region in the competition will not
enhance regional peace or increase ASEAN’s relevance in the disputes.
Thus, ASEAN was cautious to limit the US’s participation in the dispute
management. At the 2014 ASEAN Regional Forum, ASEAN members were
generally lukewarm towards the then US Secretary of State John Kerry’s
proposal of freezing proactive actions in the SCS (Mooney & Wroughton,
2014). In 2015, Indonesian Defence Minister Ryamizard Ryacudu (2015, as
cited in Parameswaran, 2015) stated that ‘if regional countries can manage
the SCS on their own, there is no need to involve others’. Moreover, when
former US President Trump offered to mediate the dispute in 2017,
Vietnam cautiously responded to his offer and the Philippines’ President
Duterte said that the dispute was ‘better left untouched’ (Shi & Liu, 2017).
ASEAN’s actions of limiting the US’ involvement in the disputes somewhat
met China’s demand for avoiding external powers’ ‘disturbances’
(Xinhua, 2013).
The COC talks began to accelerate from July 2016, probably because
China had consolidated sovereignty claims by completing land feature reclamations and declining the awards of the SCS arbitral tribunal. Some analysts view this as solid evidence of ASEAN’s weakness in managing SCS
disputes. Panda (2015) argues that only after China is ‘satisfied with its position in the SCS disputes’ can the ASEAN-driven approach progress.
Likewise, Zhang (2018, p.78) argues that ASEAN managed SCS disputes by
‘making pragmatic compromise under the principles’. In other words,
ASEAN insisted on pushing China into COC negotiation, representing the
organization’s unswerving principle (Zhang, 2018). However, facing China’s
advantageous hard power, ASEAN made pragmatic compromises by tolerating China changing the status quo to proceed with the negotiations
(Zhang, 2018).
Although this article recognises that China’s advantageous hard power
over ASEAN is one reason that ASEAN reluctantly accepted China’s land reclamation activities, this is not the only reason. First, China was not the only
claimant country that reclaimed and built outposts on land features from
2013 to 2016. For instance, in this period, Vietnam reclaimed several land
features and built military facilities on them, such as Sandy Cay and West
London Reef (Pincus, 2015). In 2014, the Philippines allocated money to

THE PACIFIC REVIEW

135

upgrade airfields and navy ports in its occupied Thitu Island (Pincus, 2015).
Accordingly, the then US Defence Minister Ash Carter recognised that “it’s
true that almost all nations that claim parts of SCS have developed outposts
over the years … of different scope and degree” (Pincus, 2015). Like Carter,
some analysts may argue that China ‘had gone much further and faster
than any other’ (Pincus, 2015). However, the others’ outpost developments
provided China with an excuse to reject the criticisms and ask ASEAN to
obey the principle of not taking sides in the disputes.
Second, while consolidating sovereignty claims, China was restrained to
prevent frictions in the SCS from spiralling out of control and breaking
down the ASEAN-China relationship. China did not use force to seize land
features held by other claimants and only reclaimed the land features that
it already occupied. Moreover, China did not cross any red line established
by the Philippines’ Duterte administration, such as not building on the
Scarborough Shoal (Viray, 2018). Further, aside from China’s de facto control over the Scarborough Shoal in 2012, China often moderated when
other claimant states strongly reacted to its actions. In 2014, China
deployed an oil rig in waters that Vietnam claimed were within its EEZ,
while China claimed the location was between the EEZs of the Paracel
Islands and Vietnam. The deployment caused a strong response from
Vietnam, which sent smaller vessels and fishing boats to confront China
(Thayer, 2016b). China then moderated by withdrawing its oil rig and sending senior diplomat Yang Jiechi to reconcile with Vietnam (Thayer, 2016b).
By being restrained, China has tried to act within ASEAN members’ limits
and maintain a space for dialogue with ASEAN.
Some analysts may also highlight ASEAN’s weakness in managing the
disputes because it conceded to China’s demand for concluding a nonlegally binding COC. However, ASEAN’s bottom-line regarding COC is binding rather than legally binding because some ASEAN states believe that no
independent world court truly exists and can punish disobedient behaviours (Cayetano, 2017, as cited in Esmaquel, 2017). China had once been
ambiguous about whether the code should be binding. For instance, in
2017, Chinese Foreign Minister Wang Yi stated that whether the code was
binding or not should be decided through ASEAN-China discussions (CCTV
News, 2017). However, China conceded to ASEAN’s demand for a binding
code. In 2019, Wang states that China will conclude a ‘final and binding
COC’ with ASEAN countries (CGTV, 2019).
To sum up, this article finds that both sceptics and proponents’ arguments concerning ASEAN’s power and external effectiveness are flawed,
because the ASEAN approach achieved some objectives and conceded
others. From 2011 to 2017, through exerting a strong and persistent diplomatic pressure on China and getting support from the US, ASEAN achieved

136

L. HU

three essential goals. First, ASEAN successfully gained formal recognition
from China as a legitimate player in SCS dispute management. Second,
China agreed with the ASEAN-driven COC approach despite its previous
insistence on a bilateral approach. Third, ASEAN defended its bottom-line
of concluding a binding COC despite China’s reluctance. However, as the
most powerful claimant state, China has important leverage in shaping the
COC because the COC would not be effective without its participation.
ASEAN did not attain its goal of early COC conclusion and conceded to
China’s non-legally binding code demand. Moreover, due to ASEAN’s
contradictory shared interests, the organization was cautious in involving
the US in the disputes, which somewhat met China’s demand to exclude
US involvement in the disputes.
Finally, ASEAN failed to prevent China from reclaiming land features.
Three factors caused this failure. First, China has hard power advantages
over ASEAN. Second, other claimants’ reclamations of or build-up of outposts on the disputed land features gave China an excuse to ask ASEAN
not to take sides. Third, China cautiously acted within ASEAN’s red lines to
maintain a space for reconciliation.

The legalistic and adversarial posturing approach versus the
ASEAN approach for managing SCS disputes
As noted in the analytical framework section, a comparison between the
ASEAN approach and the legalistic and adversarial posturing approach
helps to ascertain which approach is more effective in pushing China into
SCS dispute management and thus facilitates ASEAN’s goal attainment.
China’s reaction to the Philippines’ 2013 SCS disputes submission to the
arbitration tribunal and its proceedings and awards is an illustrative case
that can demonstrate whether a legalistic approach can constrain China
and realise ASEAN’s shared interests. This article will not engage in a normative debate over whether China should accept the awards but will
instead focus on empirically analysing whether the legalistic approach
is effective.
The 2016 SCS disputes tribunal awards challenge most of China’s sovereignty and maritime rights claims. To list a few, the tribunal ruled that
China’s historical maritime rights claims within the nine-dashed lines are
invalid (PH v. PRC, 2016). Second, it ruled that all Spratly land features and
the Scarborough shoal are not islands and thus cannot generate EEZs (PH
v. PRC, 2016).
China rejected the awards entirely. Chinese State Councillor Yang Jiechi
(2016, as cited in Xinhua Agency, 2016) states that ‘the awards are invalid
and illegal’. Yang argues that the Philippines distorted the United Nations

THE PACIFIC REVIEW

137

Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) to pursue self-interests and
harm China’s legal sovereignty rights (Yang, 2016, as cited in Xinhua
Agency, 2016). China’s view of the arbitration is also linked to its perception
that it was disadvantaged in the SCS disputes. In contrast to China’s claim
over the entire Spratly Islands, China occupies 7 land features in Spratly,
much less than Vietnam’s 27 and one less than the Philippines’ 8 (Fravel,
2012). Thus, Liu Huaqing (2004), the Chinese Navy Commander from 1972
to 1992, was angered that other claimants occupy most of the land features
in Spratly, which strategically and militarily disadvantage China. Due to this
perception, some Chinese scholars argue that some ASEAN claimants wish
to use a so-called legalistic approach to unfairly compel China to recognise
the disadvantaged fait accompli in the SCS (An, 2017). The arbitration probably solidifies this view.
Moreover, for China, the Philippines’ SCS disputes submission reflects
‘adversarial posturing’. First, in 2013, the Philippines ignored China’s lobby
that China was willing to restart the COC negotiations if the Philippines
retracted its submission (Thayer, 2013). Second, although China expressed
strong and repeated opposition to the tribunal through public statements
and note verbales in 2013, the arbitration process still moved forward
(Fravel, 2017). Thus, China thought that the tribunal ‘ignored its solemn
and just position’ (Yang, 2016, as cited in Xinhua Agency, 2016). Third,
China viewed the arbitration as a plot to ‘stigmatise and isolate China in
international society’ (Yang, 2016, as cited in Xinhua Agency, 2016).
In response to the arbitration and its awards, in 2013, Chinese President
Xi decided to reclaim the land features in Spratly to ‘fundamentally reverse
the strategic trend in the SCS’ (Xuexi Shibao, 2017). Also, at the 2016 meeting between Duterte and Xi Jinping, Xi reportedly threatened to go to war
if the Philippines tried to enforce the awards (Mogato, 2017). Moreover, in
2020, the US Secretary of State Pompeo issued a statement supporting the
tribunal awards, which triggered strong criticism from China and further
deteriorated the Sino-US relationship (Pamuk et al.,, 2020).
China’s response shows that ASEAN is unable to realise its member
states’ shared interests through a legalistic and adversarial posturing
approach. The approach dramatically destabilised the region and may
intensify the Sino-US competition. Moreover, if ASEAN had insisted on
enforcing the awards, China would have been more assertive in defending
its sovereignty claims and the ASEAN-China relationship would have
deteriorated.
In contrast, ASEAN’s non-confrontational approach prevented tensions in
the SCS from further escalating. For instance, after Pompeo issued the
statement on 13 July 2020, Chinese Foreign Minister Wang Yi had a virtual
talk the next day with his Philippines counterpart Locsin to ensure that the

138

L. HU

Philippines would cooperatively manage SCS disputes with China
(Rocamora, 2020). For China, this assurance probably indicates that the
Philippines will not side with the US. Moreover, on the same day, Chinese
President Xi called his Thailand and Singapore counterparts Prayut and Lee
respectively to obtain similar reassurances (Xinhua Net, 2020). ASEAN’s nonconfrontational approach avoided pushing China into a corner, thereby
preventing it from taking increasingly assertive actions to defend its sovereignty. Moreover, as demonstrated in the last section, although ASEAN did
not fully attain its goals, the ASEAN approach had compelled China to recognise the bloc’s role as a legitimate dispute manager and to accept the
ASEAN-driven binding COC approach.
Some analysts may argue that the COC is actually a victory for China and
serves China’s interests very well. This article recognizes that China will gain
many benefits from concluding the COC. China may want to use the code
to ‘extricate itself from’ the arbitral awards (Takahashi, 2019). Also, China
may wish to limit US involvement in the disputes by completing the code.
Moreover, China may wish to win ASEAN over in the US-China competition
by completing the code.
However, China’s victory does not necessarily mean that ASEAN will not
also gain benefits from the code. This is because an acceptable negotiation
result is that all negotiating parties gain benefits and make concessions.
The assumptions of the China victory argument are probably that contrary
to ASEAN’s expectation, the COC/ASEAN approach cannot truly constrain
China and that China will ensure its own COC version rather than making
substantive concessions to ASEAN in the COC negotiation.
Regarding the criticism that the ASEAN approach lacks a constraining
effect on China, Beeson (2020, p.579) argues that ‘China seems to be able
to live quite comfortably with an ASEAN style diplomacy that its adherents
are under no obligation to comply with, and yet which confers fig leaf legitimacy’. While the COC is under negotiation, China’s conformity with ASEAN
multilateralism can be deduced from examining the country’s obedience to
the DOC. Many scholars agree that after the DOC was signed in 2002, the
SCS was generally peaceful until 2008 (Fu & Wu, 2016; Jones & Jenne, 2016;
Storey, 2012).
Jones and Jenne (2016, p.228) argue that Beijing’s signing of the DOC in
2002, and its shift from ‘frown’ to ‘smile’ diplomacy from 1998 to 2008
stemmed from its wish to limit US involvement in the disputes when China
was weak. Storey (2012) argues that China’s cooperation with ASEAN was just
lip service because Beijing made the DOC vague and non-binding in 2002
and was lukewarm in establishing the guidelines for implementing the DOC.
This article disagrees with Storey (2012) and Jones and Jenne (2016)
argument that China just paid lip service to the DOC or the ASEAN-driven

THE PACIFIC REVIEW

139

approach. To be sure, Storey (2012) is right to argue that China was lukewarm in making progress on formalising the binding code in this period.
However, being lukewarm in completing the guideline does not mean that
the DOC did not constrain China. Contrary to Jones and Jenne (2016) argument, the period from 2002 to 2007 should have been a good time for
China to consolidate its claims in the SCS if the DOC had not restrained the
country. From 2001 to 2007, as the US was preoccupied with its war on terror, conflicts over rocks or maritime rights in the SCS probably ranked
rather low on its agenda. Thus, China did not need to deter the US’ involvement in the disputes by signing the DOC. Moreover, while China was not
strong and had a national policy of keeping a low profile from 2002 to
2008, it was stronger than the ASEAN states and could use less provocative
means to consolidate sovereignty. For instance, China could have delayed
the negotiation process of the DOC in 2002 after the US was already distracted with the 911 terrorist attack in 2001. While Storey (2012) argues
that the DOC was vague, it was better for China to not sign the DOC,
because the DOC would restrain the country from seizing unoccupied land
features in the SCS. China’s need to trade with Southeast Asian countries
may explain its obedience to the DOC from 2002 to 2008. However, at the
time, many Southeast Asian countries had just recovered from the 1997
financial crisis and thus needed China more than China needed them. Thus,
without US constraints on China’s hard power during this period, China
should have hurried up its consolidation of sovereignty in the SCS if it had
just paid lip service to the DOC or ASEAN-led multilateralism. Jones and
Jenne (2016) view China’s increasingly assertive actions from 2009 onwards
as evidence that the country abandoned its superficial commitment to
ASEAN-led multilateralism as it became stronger. However, to make such an
argument, one would have to consider the spiral of tension between China
and other claimant countries since 2009. From China’s perspective, the
actions were not an abandonment of the DOC but a response to other
claimants’ disobedience of the DOC (Fravel, 2012; Fu & Wu, 2016). If a vague
and non-binding DOC could constrain China, it is reasonable to assume
that the binding COC will have a greater constraining effect on China.
Regarding China’s concessions, to be sure, China will probably not concede to ASEAN’s every demand. However, existing evidence suggests that
China has made several substantive concessions in the COC negotiation.
Some scholars suspected that China would ensure a non-binding COC
(Castro, 2020; Storey, 2017). However, as noted earlier, although China did
not accept a legally binding COC, it conceded to ASEAN’s demand for a
binding COC. Moreover, according to the Philippine Foreign Secretary
Teodoro Locsin, Jr (2019, as cited in Santos, 2019) in 2019, in the COC negotiation, China ‘softened its insistence on controversial provisions’, such as

140

L. HU

giving up ‘provisions excluding the western military presence’. This is also a
concession that some scholars suspected that China would not make
(Castro, 2020; Hoang, 2020).

Conclusion
This article presents a systematic analysis of ASEAN’s effectiveness in managing SCS disputes and many of its findings challenge the dominant pessimistic views. First, the article finds that ASEAN members do have shared
interests and a common policy regarding SCS dispute management.
Second, China’s influence on individual ASEAN members is not as strong as
sceptics often argue. Third, ASEAN’s external effectiveness is medium. It
achieved some essential goals but also made important compromises.
ASEAN’s diplomatic pressure and utilisation of US power successfully
ASEANised the disputes and compelled China to accept the COC negotiation and conclude the binding COC. However, because China is the most
powerful claimant state and ASEAN’s use of US power has limitations,
ASEAN conceded to China’s demand to slow the COC negotiations and
reduce US involvement in the disputes. Moreover, compared with a legalistic and adversarial posturing approach, the ASEAN-driven approach is more
effective in managing the disputes.
