1. Introduction
The South China Sea has been a flashpoint in the Indo-Pacific region since the 1970 s, and while tensions continue to escalate in recent years, it remains unclear what exactly has changed or not changed over time. The case is worth studying because the nature of this maritime and territorial dispute has clearly shifted from a state-to-state disagreement to a complex web of competing claims involving multiple states. In the 2023 report on the State of Southeast Asia, the unsettled disputes with China are noted as “the biggest potential flashpoints affecting the region” [86]. Considering that approximately 39% of 460 possible maritime boundaries in the world remain under dispute as of the end of 2020 [66], it is beneficial for academics and policymakers to obtain a systematic picture of the different actions and strategies taken by contesting parties and third parties.
The dispute involves a total of six distinct political entities: Brunei Darussalam, the People’s Republic of China (referred to as China hereafter), Malaysia, the Republic of China (referred to as Taiwan hereafter), the Philippines, and Vietnam. They have competing claims over some or all of the islets, rocks and archipelagic features in the Spratly and Paracel Islands. Additionally, while Indonesia is formally a non-claimant in the South China Sea dispute, this research positions it as a ‘non-claimant disputant’ in the South China Sea because Indonesia’s Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ) intersects with China’s nine-dash demarcation. The presence of Indonesia is counted among the disputants in consideration of its recurrent clashes with China over fishing rights near the Natuna Islands. It is worth noting that on August 28, 2023, China’s Ministry of Natural Resources published the “new standard” map with ten-dash line that repeats China’s nine-dash line claim in the South China Sea while also laying claims to the east of the island of Taiwan and to Inda’s north [46].
It is important to note that the legitimacy of the Chinese claim, best known as the “U-shaped line” or the “nine-dash line,” is heavily contested because it lacks historical basis and has too much ambiguity. In a historical survey of maps and lines in the South China Sea from 1500 to 1949, Hayton [33] points out that official Chinese maps published, whether at national, regional or local, in 1760, 1784, 1866 and 1897 all showed Hainan Island as the southernmost point of Chinese territory. The basis for China’s sovereignty claims over the Paracel Islands was said to be a three-week expedition to the Paracels authorized by the Governor of Guangdong in May 1909, after which a new map of Guangdong was issued with the Paracels counted as a part of the province [33], [34]. As Hayton [33] notes, the boundary line on the 1914 map, which was kept in maps published throughout the 1920 s and early 1930 s, “represented the extent of Chinese state ‘control’ before 1736 and, significantly, the only islands within the line were Pratas and the Paracels” (p. 53).
Over the years, however, the resolution of the dispute has become even more complicated with the growing involvement of non-disputant states. For instance, the U.S. frequently conducts freedom of navigation operations, known as FONOPs, in the South China Sea despite diplomatic protests from China. Likewise, Japan, India, and Australia, all being distant outsiders and close partners to the U.S., are increasingly taking part in the regional geopolitical struggle, either through forming a security partnership (the Quad), maintaining an oil and gas exploration project with a claimant party (e.g., India’s repeated extension for an oil block offshore Vietnam), donating patrol boats (e.g., Japan’s donation of patrol watercrafts to the Philippine Coast Guard), or holding joint naval exercises (e.g., the August 2023 military drill involving Australian, U.S., and Filipino forces). It is undeniable that the waterway is a delicate front in the U.S.-China rivalry.
Against this backdrop, the study provides an updated take on the political landscape in the South China Sea from a new perspective: visualizations of times-series data between 2010 and 2022 regarding the administrative, diplomatic, economic, military, and maritime actions taken by disputants and major non-disputants. The empirical data, though subject to suggestions for improvements, is an original product aimed at identifying trends and key developments in the dispute that could facilitate conflict negotiations and management in the long run.
The paper is structured as follows: the Materials and method section introduces the data collection rationale, a coding manual, and its limitations; then, the Descriptive results section presents an overview of the empirical data, including event trends and presence of political entities. Based on the data, the Discussion section then makes two arguments: (i) there is a growing combativeness among both disputants and non-disputants in the South China Sea, with partnership initiatives and joint exercises largely in the form of competing factions, rather than a collaborative collective, and (ii) despite the façade of annual diplomatic meetings and calls for peaceful measures, disputant actors are taking actions unilaterally nearly half of the time, contributing to writing new rules in the contested sea.
2. Materials and method
2.1. Rationale
The construction of the dataset on notable non-incident events in the South China Sea for the period between 2010 and 2022 is an original work that spanned eight months total, from January 2 to August 16, 2023. This is a work in progress and aims to be released to the research community, serving those interested in examining the chain of events and trends in the South China Sea disputes. “Non-incident events” refer to events that are not characterized as unplanned encounters at sea among relevant vessels in the South China Sea. For example, non-incident events include planned military exercises, diplomatic engagements, infrastructure development, resource exploration, policy announcements, environmental initiatives, etc. The purpose is to look for non-incidental events that are not mere maritime accidents or incidents involving vessels but also other kinds of developments not at sea, such as in administrative, economic, diplomatic, military, and non-military/maritime domains.
The basis for data collection is the conceptual framework presented as Fig. 1. In this line of reasoning, the establishment of a maritime order is hinged on two factors: (i) the creation and maintenance of clear and coherent maritime regulations, and (ii) the effectiveness of mechanisms employed in dispute resolution. This is because the construction of order in the maritime domain relies on not only political adjustments founded on social consent over time [54], but also the formation of “common understood standards of behavior” [90]. Accordingly, the conceptual framework places the stabilization of state behaviors across five realms – administrative, diplomatic, economic, military, and non-military/maritime – as the driving force behind the two factors mentioned earlier. It is crucial to note that these relationships are not purely linear and unidirectional, as modifications to international rules or laws can also influence state conduct, as highlighted by Morton [54].

In light of the emergence of new maritime powers such as China, the study acknowledges the pivotal role played by changing power dynamics among naval powers in the South China Sea as well as takes into account a dynamic conception of order. This is especially pertinent given the increasing recognition of the South China Sea as a focal point for norm contestation among various political entities, encompassing from disputing and non-disputing parties, to great powers and non-great powers. Strating [90] defines norm contestation as “discursive and non-discursive challenges directed at existing norms, including non-compliance, justificatory statements and rejecting legal decisions.” The South China Sea, therefore, serves as an illustration of Chinese revisionism and a challenger to the prevailing regional order. However, it is important to clarify that this study does not exclusively focus on Chinese behaviors within the disputed sea but rather examines a spectrum of behaviors exhibited by both disputants and non-disputants over an extensive timeframe.
A major reason for constructing this kind of dataset is the lack of available data on such an important geopolitical hotspot. There is a dozen of well-known datasets about conflict, war and military security, such as (i) the Uppsala Conflict Data Program Datasets [20], (ii) the Inter-University Consortium for Political and Social Research (ICPSR) Conflict, Aggression, Violence, Wars, (iii) the Correlates of War project, (iv) the International Crisis Behavior Project, and (v) SIPRI Databases. Yet, for scholars who want to specifically examine the maritime and territorial conflict in the South China Sea, there is only a few options. The closest tool is the Maritime Awareness Project, which is maintained by the U.S. National Bureau of Asian Research (NBR), for it has interactive maps and incident timeline pertinent to the South and East China Sea disputes. A notable study resulted from the NBR project is a special report on the dynamics of assertiveness between China, the Philippines, and Vietnam [16] and an analysis into the continuity and change of China’s assertiveness from 1970 to 2015 [15]. This is perhaps the first project to provide time-series events data stretching forty-five years and identify the year 2007 as the point where one could mark key changes in the behaviors of China, such as its consolidation of administrative control and increased frequency of non-military coercion. However, it is unclear whether the time-series dataset has been continually updated post-2015, a period that also witnesses tensions in the South China Sea rising because of various factors.
2.2. Data collection process
Data collection for non-incident events in the South China Sea revolves around searching multiple news databases, namely LexisNexis and Google News, for news items on the “South China Sea” and other names of the sea in Vietnamese (“Biển Đông”) and in Chinese (“南中国海” or “南海”). For instance, to find what happened in the South China Sea in January 2010, the search would set the timeframe as: from January 1, 2010, to January 31, 2010, and so on for each month of the 2010–2022 period. Next, the author read through the search results, including headlines and blurbs to identify relevant items and extract key information in line with the pre-defined variables (Appendix 1 and Appendix 2.). Subsequently, the author coded each line of events and simultaneously kept track of unusual events in a separate note. These steps are presented in Fig. 2.

As the process was laborious, the author also took a fourth and final step to note down events that appeared unusual or remarkable in a separate column from the existing variables. For example, in 2010, China conducted three major naval exercises, the first of which was between April 10 and 24 and involved the long-range deployment of sixteen warships from the People’s Liberation Army Navy drawn from the North Sea, East Sea and South Sea Fleets. The event was marked in the Note column as a point where, “up until this exercise China’s South Sea Fleet was the only fleet to operate in the South China Sea” [92], [93]. In the following two exercises in the South China Sea, respectively on July 26–30 and November 3, China continued to flex its naval power, with the second noted as “the most notable for its large scale—biggest of its kind to date, with media coverage of live missile firings” [93].
Please see Appendix 1.1. for a systematic look at the coding manual for different variables. To sum up, there are 9 descriptive variables for event dates, publication date, political actors involved, article title, article blurb, the sublocation and location of the event, and the hyperlink addresses. Appendix 1.2. summarizes the coding of five categories: economic, administrative, diplomatic, military, and non-military/maritime. For more detailed tracking, each event is subsequently coded into smaller subjects, which total 28, such as: “administrative move” for generic actions pertaining to this category, “diplomatic meeting” for diplomatic interactions between states, territories or organizations, “construction” for the physical buildup of features in the disputed areas, etc.
2.3. Data justification and limitations
The present dataset is a work in progress, one that would be beneficial to the research community if it continues to be updated on an annual basis. The project was conceived and completed following the preliminary conclusion of a dataset on maritime incidents in the South China Sea, the early results of which have been published [61]. There are five major points to note in the use of this dataset.
First, while these categories provide a structured framework for classifying events in the South China Sea, some events might overlap between categories or have elements that span multiple categories. Nonetheless, in order to not obfuscate the dataset, the author decides to assign each line of event with only one category. It is here that the subject variable expands on and provides more clarity for the single category assigned. For example, on May 18, 2011, China Mobile announced that its mobile phone coverage had been extended to the Spratly Islands. The event is categorized as “administrative” with “construction” coded in Subject [99]. However, another event reported on May 24, 2011 in which China had set up military garrisons and outposts in the Spratly Islands would be categorized as “military” with “construction” coded in Subject [21]. Here, the subject might be the same, but the nature of the event differs. By comparison, an event categorized as “administrative” and coded as “administrative move” would be something such as the renaming of disputed features in the South China Sea. On May 3, 2012, the Office of the President of the Philippines declared that it was unilaterally renaming the disputed Scarborough Shoal as “Panatag Shoal” (which is known as Huangyan Island 黄岩岛 in Chinese and “Bãi cạn Scarborough” in Vietnamese). The Philippines, which also calls the shoal by another name, “Bajo de Masinloc,” acknowledges that this is not an island nor part of the Spartlys, yet insists that the feature lies within the 200-nautical miles of its EEZ and 200-nautical miles of its continental shelf [77]. Such coding allows the author to capture the overall trend of developments in the South China Sea while also keep tab of the different contexts of each action.
Second, similar to the limitations presented in the collection of maritime incident data, the author acknowledges that non-incident events are also subject to biased reporting from different news outlets in both local and international media as well as the author’s limited time and foreign-language ability. Reading through the amount of news articles on the South China Sea in English, Vietnamese, and Chinese over a ten-year period was an arduous task given the large volume of reporting. Reading through every news article by a single author is not possible, considering that a raw search for the key word “South China Sea” on LexisNexis for the 2010–2022 period yields 243,188 items, an overwhelming number of which would be duplicates. The method for effective reading and recording of reported events was to (i) set the search for English-language news on “South China Sea” for one month at a time on both LexisNexis and Google News, (ii) go through the search results on Google News first because the list of results tends to be more manageable, and then (iii) cross check the results with LexisNexis afterward. This process was repeated until data for every month from 2010 to 2022 was collected.
Third, despite an effort to be as thorough as possible about the data collection, it is undeniable that the dataset is non-exhaustive, subject to subjectivity, limited expertise, error and oversight, and should be checked again by a third-party once it is publicized. The quality of the dataset can be improved with a data validity test as the current one has only one coder. The research acknowledges that not all maritime incidents and non-incidents are reported in the public domain, and thus certain events, such as encounters between navies that may be considered to be “routine,” do not appear in the dataset.
Fourth, the current coding system is a simplified one that focuses on identifying the stakeholders and the type of events taking place. In future work, considering that at the most basic level, nations choose between cooperation and conflict, the coding method could thus be expanded to study the level of aggressiveness of the political entities being tracked, such as in Chubb [15], [16], or the different levels of cooperation, if any, for events that include more than two political entities.
Lastly, the data collection begins with 2010 for three reasons. One, events earlier than 2010 are hard to collect and compile systematically due to the lack of available resources. Two, the period after 2010 saw a growing involvement of the U.S. in the South China Sea, so much that the contested sea is no longer a battleground for the disputants but also for the non-disputants, including Japan, Australia, and India. And three, existing datasets of this kind are not publicized and not being updated to the latest timeline.
3. Descriptive results
3.1. Event characteristics
In total, between 2010 and 2022, there are 1099 events spanning five categories, of which 104 are administrative, 188 diplomatic, 105 economic, 510 military, and 192 non-military/maritime by nature. Fig. 3 shows the bar chart of events in the South China Sea on a yearly basis. Please note that the “non-military/maritime” category is shortened as “maritime” in Fig. 3 for visual clarity purpose. On average, about 84 events were recorded annually for the ten-year period, which means at least one notable event would take place a week in the disputed sea.

Three trends can be interpreted from Fig. 3. First, events of military nature (510) still dominate when taking into account the combination of diplomatic and maritime events (380). This could be due to a tendency by mainstream media to report on military events, which can be more sensational and newsworthy than non-military events, as well as a possible case of militarization in the sea. Second, while hydrocarbon and fishery interests, considered of economic nature, may be an important component of every disputant’s claim in the South China Sea, economic events are not noticeably as big as those of military or maritime nature in the past decade. Third, the number of actions taken since 2018 appears to be increasing and is consistently higher than the ten-year average, suggesting tensions have indeed escalated in the region in recent years.

It is also noteworthy that, not only does the number of unilateral events appear to be higher than those of bilateral and multilateral events, the number of military events, both unilateral and bilateral, also seems to be rising in recent years. Meanwhile, other than the annual ASEAN summits and regional forum meetings, there is not much happening on the diplomatic front among the disputants.

3.2. Presence of political entities
To look more closely at who among the disputants and non-disputants have been the most active, the treemap in Fig. 5 displays the hierarchical count of political entities presence as a set of nested rectangles. Among the seven disputants, China, Vietnam, and the Philippines were the biggest actors, while among the major non-disputants, the U.S., Japan, and Russia were the most vocal. Yet, notably, the U.S. showed a bigger presence in the South China Sea than even Vietnam and the Philippines. Two reasons could be attributed to this phenomenon: (i) English-language news might be U.S.-centric and capture more of the actions taken by the U.S., and (ii) since the Obama administration’s pivot to Asia was launched in December 2011, the U.S. has indeed directed more resources and efforts to the Asia-Pacific, including the South China Sea.

To further highlight the expansive presence of China and the U.S. in the contested waterway, the following sub-sections detail the involvement into (i) event categories and (ii) groups of disputant and non-disputant entities over time.
3.2.1. Disputants
Several observations can be made from Fig. 6 and Fig. 7. First, China has been taking more military actions and generally on a unilateral basis. Second, both Vietnam and the Philippines appear to be taking more actions on a bilateral basis than the rest of the disputants, though the latter’s involvement is notably more militaristic in nature. Third, Taiwan’s actions are highly militaristic and unilateral, particularly after 2018. Fourth, while both Indonesia and Malaysia are also engaged in a high number of military events, these appear to be of both bilateral and multilateral nature. Fifth, Brunei, long known as the “silent claimant,” is unsurprisingly the least vocal political entity in the dispute. It is worth noting that, among the claimant states, although Brunei claims Bombay Castle, Louisa Reef, Owen Shoal, and Rifleman Bank in southern Spratlys on the ground that these features fall within Brunei’s EEZ (as declared in 1984), it is the only country to neither declare sovereignty over these features nor maintain any military outpost there. Of these features, Vietnam currently occupies Bombay Castle and Rifleman Bank, while Malaysia seems to have rescinded its claim over Louisa Reef in a bilateral agreement with Brunei in 2009 [[19], [89]]. Brunei’s reticence can be pointed to the fact that (i) it has one of the weakest armed forces in Southeast Asia in terms of both size and capability, and that (ii) Brunei insists that the mechanism for conflict resolution in the South China Sea be bilateral and in line with the not-yet-finalized ASEAN-China Code of Conduct.

3.2.2. Non-disputants
As pointed out earlier in Fig. 4, non-disputants get involved more in diplomatic and military events. Fig. 8 zooms in on the six non-disputants that are the most active in the South China Sea over the examined decade. Here it becomes clear that these outsider states have been watching and getting their navies more involved in the region since 2011. The U.S. is taking the lead with military actions at all levels (198 military events over its 286 events, or 69%), especially unilateral (93 over 198, or nearly 47%) and bilateral (82 over 198, or 41.4%). Japan comes second with 102 recorded events, of which the military kind amounts to 62 events, or 60.7%, with 44 events being bilateral and 15 being multilateral. By comparison, Russia is present in 48 events, of which 30 are military events (62.5%). Within these, 27 (or 90%) are bilateral military events and 2 are multilateral. Among the six major powers, India appears to be the only state to not have exhibited a consistent level of engagement in the South China Sea, understandably because it does not have any regional allies, such as the relationships the U.S. maintains with the Philippines. There was not a considerable number of notable news involving India in the sea between 2015 and 2019. Nonetheless, with the re-establishment in 2017 of the Quadrilateral Security Dialogue, shortened as the Quad, between Australia, India, Japan, and the U.S., it is expected that India would align with its strategic partners on the topic of the South China Sea, hence, its more vocal presence after 2019.

3.2.2.1. What should be noted concerning the role of the U.S.?
The U.S.’ shifting policy on the South China Sea could be dated to 2010, with a couple of noteworthy decisions in the subsequent years that contributed to projecting U.S. power in the region as well as exacerbating, rather than alleviating, tensions. The first turning point is on July 23, 2010, when then U.S. Secretary of State Hillary Rodham Clinton, speaking at a regional security forum in Hanoi, said “the United States has a national interest” in resolving the claims [2]. China’s Ministry of Foreign Affairs made no immediate comment at the time, though its then Foreign Minister Yang Jiechi did reiterate Beijing’s long-standing position that the disputes to not be internationalized. As Strating [90] points out, China’s “strategic ambiguity” approach to the South China Sea, evidenced by its submission of the nine-dash line map in a 2009 note verbale to the Commission on the Limits of the Continental Shelf (CLCS) under the United Nations, has led to heightened security concerns among not just the claimants but also the U.S. and its ally Japan. In June 2010, the U.S. became the first non-ASEAN country to set up a dedicated Mission to ASEAN in Jakarta. This argument departs from a previous analysis which pinpoints the turning point in U.S. policy on the South China Sea to be the 2013–2015 period—when China had stepped up island reclamation and construction for military purposes [60]. More precisely, the rise of China since 2009 had already factored into Washington’s shifting interest in the region, hence its announcement of a rebalance to the Asia-Pacific in late 2011.
The second point is in October 2012 when the supercarrier USS George Washington of the U.S. Navy not only entered the South China Sea but also hosted a delegation of Vietnamese officials aboard the ship [3]. While the first U.S. FONOP is often known as the deployment on October 27, 2015, of the USS Lassen (DDG-82) within 12 nautical miles of Subi Reef — off the western coast of the Philippines, the peacetime patrols of U.S. warships are not new and part of the Freedom of Navigation Program established in 1979 to challenge maritime claims the U.S. finds inconsistent with international law [63]. In July 2012, the USS George Washington was also in the South China Sea for a vertical replenishment at sea, but only since late 2015 has the U.S. become more active in exercising its navigational rights and freedom in the area through transit operations. As part of the Obama administration’s strategic re-focus on Asia, the U.S. had planned from 2012 to reposition 60% of its naval fleet, up from about 50% then, to the Pacific by 2020 [1]. Between 2015 and 2022, the U.S. completed 44 FONOPs in the South China Sea, of which 15 were near the Spratlys, 17 near the Paracels, and others in not clearly identified areas.
What has followed is increased cooperation and assistance between the U.S. and South China Sea disputant parties from the mid-2010 s onward. Growing U.S. involvement in the region can be observed through the annual Cooperation Afloat Readiness and Training (CARAT) Exercises and Southeast Asia Cooperation and Training (SEACAT) Exercises, in addition to helping Vietnam, Malaysia, the Philippines, and Indonesia improve their maritime domain awareness (MDA). The International Maritime Organization (IMO) defines MDA as: “[t]he effective understanding of any activity associated with the maritime environment that could impact security, safety, the economy or the marine environment” [40]. As Guilfoyle [31] has noted, MDA is a form of intelligence in maritime security which involves the sharing of information not just between intelligence and maritime law enforcement agencies but also between the agencies of different countries. By increasing cooperation with Southeast Asian states in MDA—an originally U.S. concept [7], [31], Washington seeks to develop a dynamic and expansive environment for maritime security. For instance, Indonesia and the United States cooperate on defense issues under the auspices of the U.S.-Indonesia Comprehensive Partnership, reaffirmed in October 2015. According to the U.S. Defense Department (2019), bilateral U.S.-Indonesia defense activities encompass more than 200 bilateral military engagements a year, plus one Joint Staff sponsored exercise, and several service-level maneuver exercises annually. Similarly, under the U.S. government’s Indo-Pacific Maritime Security Initiative, it is providing Malaysia with sensors and maritime patrol aircrafts, and Vietnam with training and security assistance, including Scan Eagle Unmanned Aerial Vehicles, T-6 trainer aircraft, a former U.S. Coast Guard high-endurance cutter. It is worth noting that, since the removal of decades-long U.S. embargo on sales of lethal weapons to Vietnam on May 23, 2016 [103], no such sales have taken place. Brunei has also been maintaining close military-to-military ties with the U.S. through participation in the annual bilateral CARAT Exercise and Rim of the Pacific (RIMPAC) exercises.
Overall, the U.S. has taken a largely bilateral and security-centered approach to the South China Sea through two major means, namely (i) strengthening military-to-military ties with disputant states other than China, and (ii) deploying more military assets to the region, including its most advanced surveillance aircraft, the P-8A Poseidon, out of the Philippines for patrols over the South China Sea.
4. Discussion
4.1. Growing combativeness amid uneven military modernization plans and limited maritime cooperation
The prominence of military activities among disputants and non-disputants over the past decade, coupled with a lack of diplomatic breakthroughs and cooperative maritime initiatives, point toward a conflict escalating in hostility intent as well as an uncertain prospect of conflict resolution. Of the 1099 notable events tracked, 510 or 46.4% mark those of military nature, with 163 being military exercises, 51 dispatches of military equipment (e.g., reconnaissance aircraft, warships, aircraft carriers, unmanned intelligent drone carrier) or an army unit (e.g., self-defense maritime militia unit), 70 events relating to the transfer, sale, or procurement of military weapons, and 34 disclosures of domestic weapon development. The numbers hint at a tendency to resort to military tactics, such as mobilizing arms units and holding military drills, rather than to engage in diplomatic negotiations. This was quite apparent when looking at the behavior of the U.S. in the sea.

One figure that best explains China’s growing military power, and hence, assertiveness, is its ability to self-produce advanced weapons and military technology within a short period. Of the 43 events coded under “military technology development,” China was responsible or 32. Between 2010 and 2012, China successfully developed, tested, and deployed the world’s first weapons system capable of targeting a moving carrier strike group (CSG) from long-range, land-based mobile launchers [17], its stealth fighter jet J-20 [10], and its first aircraft carrier Liaoning [24]. It is important to note that, for the decade prior to 2010, China was already known for focusing on the development and deployment of robust conventional short-range ballistic missile (SRBM) capabilities. As early as 2009, Erickson and Yang [25] have warned of China’s efforts to develop anti-ship ballistic missile (ASBM) capabilities with the aim of expanding control over its maritime periphery and diminish U.S. military access. The move is consistent with China’s strategic thinking of deploying land-based missiles to attack selected targets at sea from the 1970 s. In the 2017–2022 period, Chinese efforts at military modernization accelerated. It took just over two years, from April 2017 to December 2019, for China to complete building and put into service its first domestic aircraft carrier, Shandong [12]. During the same period, China claimed to have made the world’s fastest unmanned surface vehicle, dubbed Tianxing-1 [67], and the world’s first armed amphibious drone boat capable of being used in land assault operations and in a combat triad with aerial drones and other drone ships [78]. Most notably, on May 2, 2018, CNBC, citing U.S. intelligence sources, reported that China had quietly installed anti-ship cruise missiles and surface-to-air missile systems on three of its fortified outposts west of the Philippines in the South China Sea [47].
Military technology development in the rest of the disputant parties pales in comparison not just in terms of quantity but also quality. In the examined decade, none of the disputants were reported to have made any breakthrough in military technology equivalent to those in China. Most of the known reports were on either domestically-made patrol vessels or transfers of patrol vessels from the U.S. or Japan. For example, the Malaysian Maritime Enforcement Agency on March 15, 2017 received its first of six New Generation Patrol Crafts (NGPC), dubbed KM Bagan Datuk, that was considered “the largest, fastest and most sophisticated vessel to enter the enforcement agency’s fleet” [53]. Malaysia, however, found a major project to build six littoral combat ships for its Navy from 2014 to 2019 to have failed due to corruption, with no ships produced at all and approximately USD300 million misappropriated [58]. Similarly, Vietnam’s attempts to improve its domestic ship-building industry and strengthen its maritime law enforcement agency have also been hampered by limited technical financial abilities as well as corruption. In June 2023, Vietnam issued a 16-year jail sentence to a former coast guard commander for embezzling USD2 million from military procurement deals [45].
Fig. 11 and Fig. 12 highlight that, other disputant states, particularly Vietnam, the Philippines, and Indonesia, heavily depend on imports and foreign aid of maritime and military equipment from the non-disputant major powers. Of the 1099 recorded events, 172 were about arms deals, with the lead suppliers being Japan (14.5%), the U.S. (9.9%), Russia (8.1%), South Korea (8.1%). Regarding involvement in events on armed deal among the contesting parties, the Philippines took the lead with 40 (23.2%), while Vietnam came second with 25 (14.5%), Indonesia third with 8 (4.65%), and Malaysia fourth with 6 (3.48%).

Besides the active attempts by different parties to enhance their military power, evidence for the growing combativeness also lies in the low number of maritime cooperation events, whether these be joint patrols, maritime exercises geared toward humanitarian assistance/disaster relief (HA/DR) or maritime law enforcement interoperability drill, or collaborative fishery scheme and maritime research. Just 126 events (11.46%) in the 2010–2022 period related to maritime activities of non-military nature. Here, even though some maritime exercises did mark the presence of armed naval forces, the subject was coded “maritime exercise” because the news sources reported only actions such as HA/DR, cross deck flying/evacuation, lashing, a man overboard exercise (MOBEX), oil leakage control, and sports and social interaction. Remarkably, joint patrols did take place frequently but involved non-disputants more than disputants, such as between the Philippines and the U.S., or the Philippines and Australia, between just Japan and Australia, or between Vietnam and India, or Vietnam and Japan.
This is not to say that efforts to increase bilateral and multilateral cooperation in the maritime domain are non-existent. As shown in Fig. 3, Fig. 4, the number of diplomatic events rose after 2019. Taking a closer look at diplomatic meetings and signed documents in the past four years, there have indeed been increased attempts at trust building and information sharing on maritime law enforcement in Southeast Asia, but even such moves have been uneven. Within the ASEAN in general and the claimants in particular, Vietnam has inked the highest number of Memorandum of Understanding on bilateral coast guard cooperation, namely with the Philippines (2013), China (2016), Indonesia (2021), Malaysia (2022), and Thailand (2023), in addition to being present in every other regional initiative on maritime law enforcement. Vietnam was also the first to hold a four-day exchange program in 2022 for coast guard delegations from Cambodia, China, Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, and Thailand. Meanwhile, existing initiatives to break the stalemate in the South China Sea have been lackluster. For instance, the ASEAN and China have continued to hold Senior Officials’ Meeting on the Implementation of the Declaration on the Conduct of Parties (SOM-DOC) as well as meeting of their Joint Working Group on the Implementation of the Declaration on the Conduct of Parties in the South China Sea (JWG-DOC) on a regular basis over the ten years, but no practical outcome has been reached. Another example is the sluggish efforts at increasing regional cooperation in maritime law enforcement. The idea of an ASEAN Coast Guard Forum (ACGF) was first conceived back in 2015 but only until November 2022 was the first meeting of the ACGF formally held.
Fig. 13 details four kinds of diplomatic events—meeting, notable statement, partnership initiative, and port visit—in the ten political entities that are most prominent in the South China Sea over the examined period. It confirms Vietnam’s active diplomatic engagement as well as China’s prominent presence in the region’s diplomacy. Yet, with the absence of a political breakthrough in the disputes, existing diplomatic engagements appear to be rather ineffective at deterring relevant parties, whether with direct or marginal interests in the South China Sea, from further militarizing the region.

In short, the rising combative characteristic of the disputes is attributed to two factors. First, disputants and non-disputants continue to strengthen their naval and maritime enforcement agencies through different means but at an uneven rate, with China out-spending and out-performing all the other disputants. Second, existing maritime partnership initiatives, joint exercises, and diplomatic meetings have largely taken place in the form of competing factions and fallen short of agreeable outcomes. Yet, regarding China’s assertive approach to the South China Sea, one can argue it has been relatively consistent throughout the 2010–2022 period. China maintained bilateral and multilateral contacts through regularized regional forums and diplomatic channels all the while quietly invested in military technology research and development and consolidating military outposts on disputed features.
4.2. Territorialization via combination of administrative, legal, and economic actions
Another pattern of behavior observed in the dataset is a combined use of military deployment, administrative reconfiguration, legal declarations, and economic mechanisms among the disputants, to different extent, in order to assert their claims and to pressure other parties into complying with the changing status quo. In unsettled territorial and maritime disputes such as the case of the South China Sea, to gain and assert control over territorial assets, other than relying on their armed forces, disputant parties deploy a host of non-military tools such as tourism, administrative rezoning and renaming, infrastructure upgrade, fishing moratorium, and deployment of civilian forces such as marine conservationists, oil and gas survey vessels, and fishing fleet [13], [84], [102]. Although it is well acknowledged in the literature that China is using tourism and administrative management as a tactic to not only assert military and administrative control but also to cultivate patriotic sentiment at home [37], [38], [84], such territorialization tactics have not been studied sufficiently alongside with China’s actions in other domains such as maritime diplomacy, law, and economics, or in comparison with those of other disputants. Having the dataset of events from multiple domains is thus beneficial to a cross-examination of political behaviors across time. The installation of telecommunication networks in disputed islands is an interesting example. From as early as 2007, Vietnam’s military-run telecommunications company Viettel had surveyed the possibility of setting up base transceiver stations in the Spratly Islands, and by April 2010, had successfully allowed soldiers and residents stationed there to connect to the 2G network [56]. Yet, it took China only one year, from April 2010 to May 2011, to catch up with such development in the Spratly Islands, and another two years to upgrade the network to 3G for both the Spratly Islands and Paracel Islands [22], [6]. Even though as of May 2022, Viettel cellular network has covered 21 islands and 33 islets claimed by Vietnam in the Spratly Islands [55], China has outpaced Vietnam in terms of not just telecommunication provision but also civil-military infrastructure and services in the disputed islands.
In revisiting some notable chain of events, this section makes two claims. First, on the administrative and economic fronts, except for China, all other disputants are being reactive, rather than proactive, and hence, clearly lacking a grand strategy at both the state and regional level. Second, it can be argued that legal actions are primarily motivated by political considerations aimed at safeguarding one’s territorial claims while also limiting the actions of the other parties. The protracted attempt to build a COC also suggests that the primary objective of claimants is not necessarily to develop a comprehensive legal framework in the contested sea but rather to not escalate the conflict any further through maintenance of diplomatic talks.
4.2.1. Reactive responses among disputants
An in-depth look into the administrative and economic moves taken by disputant parties during the examined period reveals how disputants are taking more assertive actions largely in response to China’s military and administrative build-up in the South China Sea. This is most apparent when studying the varying extent of land reclamation and harbor building on the occupied features in the Paracel Islands and Spratly Islands. Despite the diplomatic façade of resolving their differences through “friendly consultations and negotiations,” China, Vietnam, the Philippines, and Taiwan were all found to engage in some kind of construction work, with China notably surpassing the rest in both speed and scope.
4.2.1.1. Infrastructure construction
According to the Asia Maritime Transparency Initiative (AMTI), since 2013, China has created 3200 acres of new land in the Spratlys while expanding its presence substantially in the Paracels, operating 20 outposts in the latter and 7 in the former [18]. Meanwhile, Vietnam’s construction only became noticeable recently. Since 2017, Vietnam has pushed for land reclamation at 8 of the 10 rocks it occupies in the Spratly Island chain, in addition to installing smaller outposts on submerged reefs and banks [19]. What sets China’s construction apart from Vietnam’s is the comprehensive development of an island city that, according to the satellite images, includes a school [64], a lighthouse [51], airstrips capable of accommodating fighter jets and surveillance aircraft [70], a base station for shelter and rescue operations [82], and an environmental monitoring station [42].
By comparison, construction works by the Philippines, Taiwan, and Indonesia are even more miniscule and sluggish. The Philippines, for example, has worked on only one naval base, the Oyster Bay, which is located in Palawan and only 160 km from the disputed Spratly Islands, in addition to building a beaching ramp on the Manila-controlled Thitu Island (called Pagasa Island in the Philippines). It took two years, from July 2013 to August 2015, for the Philippines to realize a plan on relocating major air force and navy camps to Subic Bay, a former U.S. naval base northwest of Manila, to gain faster access to waters being contested by China in the South China Sea [28], [57]. The re-utilization of Subic Bay is driven by two factors: the cost to repair and improve it was half the cost to build a new base, and the strategic location of the deep-water harbor on the western side of Luzon Island would allow the Philippines to station its air force and navy just opposite the South China Sea. Nonetheless, the move is deemed reactive because the Philippines was underutilizing the bay from 2009 to 2013 and likely only changed its mind given the growing assertiveness of China in the 2010–2013 period. The number of military-type events in the contested waters rose from just 5 in 2010–12 in the first six months of 2013.
The reactive responses can also be observed in recent years. In 2018, Indonesia opened a military base with 1000 personnel in Selat Lampa on Natuna Besar Island – part of the Natuna Islands, where Indonesia and China frequently clash over fishing rights. Between 2010 and 2019, there were at least 9 known maritime incidents between Indonesian and Chinese vessels near or north of the Natuna Islands, according to the author’s maritime incident tracker [61]. Taking into account the government and media discourse in Indonesia, it is fitting to argue that the frequent incursions of foreign ships, including Chinese fishing militia and naval vessels, have driven Indonesia to take a series of more assertive actions, such as (i) renaming the waters of the Indonesian EEZ area located north of the Natuna Sea as the North Natuna Sea in 2017, (ii) opening the Selat Lampa military base in 2018, (iii) increasing patrols and tighten security and defense in the North Natuna Sea [80], and (iv) considering turning the Natuna Islands into a special economic zone [44].
4.2.1.2. Tourism development
Evidence from economics-related events, including tourism development and oil and gas exploration, further confirms the domination of China and the lack of initiatives from the rest of the disputants. On January 4, 2010, just over six months after its submission of the infamous nine-dash line map to the CLCS and two years prior to its formal establishment of Sansha as a prefecture-city unit, China announced a plan to develop Hainan island for tourism in the decade to 2020 [96]. It was noted that the idea of tourism in the Spratly Islands appeared in official Chinese government planning likely for the first time in April 2013 [14]. Then, after a year of trial, on September 2, 2014, the first cruise ship departed the port of Sanya, Hainan for the Paracel Islands (known as Xisha in China), offering around 200 Chinese tourists a four-day, three-night experience, on a monthly or bimonthly basis, to the disputed destination [101]. By contrast, Vietnam and the Philippines, which considered introducing tourism services in 2015, have not been able to realize such plans as well as China did. June 2015 was when Vietnam first advertised a trip costing USD800/person for citizens to see parts of the Spratly Islands and partake in night fishing and lighthouse visit [81]. Yet, to date, no such tours have been commercialized on the scale of the Chinese cruises to the Paracels. Vietnam, however, did organize trips for Buddhist monks to the Southwest Cay and Sin Cowe Island [79], for government officials, state employees, university students, and overseas Vietnamese to visit soldiers and residents on the island district of Truong Sa (Spratly Islands) and DK1 platforms in the years after 2015. The Philippines took even longer than Vietnam to realize its plan for tourism development in the disputed islands. In August 2015, it launched a campaign for “ecotourism zone” development in the Spratlys, but only until June 2023 did it begin to offer a seven-day tour, dubbed the Great Kalayaan or Freedom Expedition, costing more than USD2000/person, to the Spratly Islands [4], [65].
The main reason for arguing that neither Vietnam nor the Philippines is as proactive as China in deploying tourism as a territorializing tactic is their lack of tourism policies for the mass over the years. Both have developed policies similar to China’s in an attempt to counter the latter’s tourism development, but none has achieved the same domestic success as in China. In fact, state-directed Chinese tourism to the Spratly and Paracel Islands is not new, Beijing has strategically employed outbound tourism as an economic tool, leveraging it to secure concessions as seen in Taiwan, South Korea, Tibet, and Hong Kong [83], [84]. On the one hand, China’s tourism operations did seem to motivate other disputants to follow suits. On the other hand, the slow and uneven development of tourism services in the South China Sea over the past decade supports the finding of Huang and Suliman [38] that a tourism war seems unlikely. Yet, it is overly optimistic of Huang and Suliman [38] to presume that tourism would not worsen the geopolitical conflicts in the South China Sea and may serve as an alternative channel for peaceful coexistence, given that no such cooperative mechanism has been put forth among the disputants and that separate tourism plans persist up to this day.
4.2.1.3. Oil and gas activities
A similar trend of reactivity among disputants is found upon examining events concerning oil and gas development. Of the 132 events coded as such, China took up 42 events (or 37%), of which 30 (or 71.4%) happened unilaterally. In comparison, Vietnam recorded 20 oil and gas development events (15%), of which only 3 were unilateral and 12 were bilateral. Further down the line are the Philippines (7 events), Indonesia (3), Brunei (3), and Malaysia (1). In particular, China was the first to accelerate efforts on offshore exploration in the South China Sea when in May 2011, China National Offshore Oil Corporation (CNOOC), the nation’s largest offshore oil producer, invited foreign bidding for joint development of 19 blocks in the South China Sea [85]. A year later, China put into service its first homegrown deep-water drilling rig in the South China Sea, marking its claim as “the first country to explore deep-water oil and gas resources” there [100]. By December 2012, amid diplomatic protests and heated maritime encounters with Vietnam and India, China’s National Energy Administration decided to name the South China Sea as the main offshore site for natural gas production [69].
The lack of development in the oil and gas sector among the claimants is caused by China’s consistent opposition to any attempts to conclude exploration agreements with international oil companies in the South China Sea. In the wake of relentless pressure from Beijing, Hanoi has incurred significant financial losses from terminating various agreements with international partners involved in oil and gas exploration within its continental shelf near the Vanguard Bank in the South China Sea.1 Coercion from China also seems to be the case when the Philippines decided in April 2022 to suspend all oil and gas exploration activities within the disputed areas in the West Philippine Sea, the Filippino name for the part of the South China Sea claimed by Manila [5]. Meanwhile, contrary to the quiet unilateral surrender of Vietnam and the Philippines on this matter, Malaysia’s state-owned Petronas and CNOOC in July 2021 signed a liquefied natural gas (LNG) supply deal estimated to be worth $7 billion over ten years. The agreement came just one month after reports of China harassing Malaysia’s offshore gas developments at the Kasawari gas field in the Malaysian part of the South China Sea in Block SK316 offshore Sarawak [26], [41].
Overall, China’s assertiveness looks particularly glaring not just because it is almost the only disputant to always take a step ahead of the others, but also because other disputants are not responding forcefully or proactively taking initiatives that could push for their claims.
4.2.2. International law as a tool in the political calculations of individual disputant
In observing legal actions taken by disputants (36 recorded), one sees that the number of unilateral decisions (30) is considerably higher than that of bilateral (3) or multilateral decisions (3). It is important to note here that attempts to negotiate and finalize a COC is not counted as “legal move,” but instead as “diplomatic meeting,” in the dataset in consideration of such talks being more diplomatic than legal by nature. As such, the legal moves recorded here consist of submissions of documents or note verbale to the United Nations. Although disputants frequently state the importance of maintaining peace, stability, and respect to the international law, in practice, legal actions continue to be driven by political interests—the inherent interests of protecting one’s territorial claims through the law, and thereby, constraining other parties—rather than the desire to establish a legal order in the maritime domain.
4.2.2.1. Submissions to the Commission on the Limits of the Continental Shelf (CLCS)
In one example, disputants were quick to protect their maritime and territorial interests via individual and joint submissions to the Commission on the Limits of the Continental Shelf (CLCS)—one of the scientific bodies created under the 1982 UNCLOS in accordance with Article 76 [98]. There are three options available to coastal states: (1) Make a final submission stating that the outer limits of the continental shelf extended beyond 200 nautical miles; (2) Provide preliminary information indicative of the outer limits of the continental shelf beyond 200 nautical miles and a description of the status of preparation and intended date of making a submission in accordance with the requirements of Article 76 of the Convention and with the Rules of Procedure and the Scientific and Technical Guidelines of the CLCS; or (3) Make a reservation of rights to the continental shelf by making objection to submitted files [59], [62], [98]. Countries that take no action will be considered to have no interest in claiming an extended continental shelf over the 200 nautical miles measured from its baseline. Given the deadline in May 2009, coastal states in the South China Sea were all quick to make their submissions, with others issuing prompt objections in response. 4.2.2.2. Negotiations on the code of conduct
Political considerations, such as wariness over upsetting a powerful China, appear to have figured prominently in the disputants’ approach to law-making in the sea. The Philippines was the only one to institute arbitral proceedings against China under Annex VII to the 1982 UNCLOS in January 2013, but even in the aftermath of winning this historic case, since 2016, the Philippines has been reluctant to use the ruling as a legal basis to challenge China’s expansive claims in the South China Sea. With that said, when it comes to any sort of regional agreement, including the completion of a COC, none of the disputants have been as quick as they were individually to push for a higher frequency of meetings of the ASEAN-China Joint Working Group, or to hold all parties accountable to the three-year timeline of building the COC. On the role of the ASEAN, Hu [36] suggests that ASEAN, through diplomatic pressure and utilizing U.S. influence, has successfully “ASEANized” the disputes, prompting China to accept the COC negotiations. However, due to China’s dominance and the limitations on U.S. involvement, concessions were made, resulting in a slowdown of COC negotiations and reduced U.S. participation at China’s request [36]. This take is interesting but seems incomplete as China’s willingness to return to the COC negotiations was not simply due to pressure from the ASEAN but also due to its changing intention. In the 2013–2016 period, China appears to have used the COC as a legal basis to exclude outside powers, rather than to establish a regional rule of law for conduct in the South China Sea.

China’s lack of willingness for multilateral cooperation on the disputes was apparent from the mid-1990 s to the early 2000 s [27], [35], [39], [43]. China’s occupation of the Mischief Reef, which was claimed by the Philippines, in 1994, was a move aimed at pressuring Manila into bilateral discussion with Beijing. Manila, however, had tried to enlist the support of other ASEAN members and major powers, thereby, regionalizing and internationalizing the disputes against China’s wish [23]. Although the signing of the DOC in 2002 marks the first time that China had participated in and given consent to a multiparty agreement on the South China Sea, the event did not build momentum for any further breakthrough [11], [52]. Another 10 years passed before the ASEAN reached six-point principles, all of which reiterated the need to comply and respect existing mechanisms and principles of international law, to exercise restraint and non-use of force, and to commit to peaceful dispute resolution.
Studies on the COC as a work in progress share the view that China has not always been supportive and constructive of such an agreement. The COC, first and foremost, is “not an instrument to settle territorial disputes or maritime delimitation issues” per a draft framework, but is set to become a rules-based framework to promote confidence-building and better manage or prevent incidents between China and Southeast Asian disputants [32], [91]. As such, disagreements lasted throughout the 2000 s with regard to a number of points, including the geographical scope, the duty to cooperate, the role of third party, and the mechanism by which consensus would be reached [94]. Storey [88] notes that, up until 2013, China had very little reason to work with the ASEAN on the COC owing to possibly three reasons: (i) the Philippines’ initiation of an arbitral proceeding in January 2013 despite China’s objection further internationalized the dispute, (ii) Beijing suspected Manila and Hanoi were using the ASEAN’s agenda for their own interests, and (iii) China wanted its views on the South China Sea to be equally included, instead of letting an ASEAN draft code be the basis of negotiations. Another argument is Beijing has the most to lose from a COC because a protocol for regional conduct could hamper Chinese activities, including island buildup and increased de facto control of disputed features through maritime patrols [97]. Additionally, Mishra [52] points to how the rising assertiveness of China since 2007–2008 has fueled nationalism in neighboring countries, resulting in disharmony between collective ASEAN position and that of individual member countries, particularly in 2011–2012.
In examining the chain of events that happened in the 2014–2016 period, including the standoff between China and Vietnam over the Hai Yang Shi You 981 oil rig in May-June 2014 and the final award of the Permanent Court of Arbitration (PCA) in July 2016, the paper argues that, just as China’s submission of the note verbales with the infamous map to the CLCS helps reinforce its position in the disputes, its changing attitude toward consultations and negotiations on the COC after 2016 is another example of Beijing strategically utilizing a regional platform to advance its political calculations. First, China needs to deflect international attention away from the PCA’s ruling, in which China was portrayed as party non-compliant with international law, from its non-participation in the arbitration process to its refusal to acknowledge the final ruling. The PCA declared that China’s nine-dash line claims violate international law, Chinese reclamation and construction projects in the disputed waters infringe on Philippines’ territorial rights, and China is guilty of destroying the maritime environment and illegally preventing Filipinos from fishing and oil exploration in the area [71]. Second, resuming talks on the COC allows China to present itself as a party willing to abide by the rules. Ha [32] notes this move falls in line with China’s embrace of multilateral diplomacy through the 2000 s as a way to assuage neighboring countries of its “peaceful rise.” Third, in actively pushing for the COC negotiation process, China could shape the development of a regional law-based framework in a direction that aligns with its broader strategic objectives in the South China Sea. For instance, it could use the COC as a legal basis to effectively exclude external interference, such as from the United States. According to the Single Draft Negotiating Text (SDNT) of the COC, not only are third parties defined as non-signatory states to the COC, they also appear to be entirely unwelcome in accession to the COC [32], [94]. Several points proposed by China concern the exclusion of third parties, such that (i) cooperation on the marine economy should be conducted by littoral states, not countries from outside the region, and (ii) joint military exercises shall not be held with countries from outside the region unless the concerned parties are notified beforehand and express no objection [32], [94].
5. Conclusion
The findings highlight two major developments over the past decade in the South China Sea. First, empirical evidence confirms a growing sense of combativeness amid uneven militarization in the region. On the one hand, the data show an inclination to resort to military tactics, such as mobilizing arms units and holding military drills, instead of engaging in diplomatic negotiations. Notably, among the non-disputants, the U.S. was found to be the most active entity in the South China Sea in the 2010–2022 period, with its presence marked as bigger than even Vietnam and the Philippines. This means the prominence of military events in the contested sea could be attributed to the military activities of the U.S., e.g., frequent military exercises with the Philippines and FONOPs. The presence of other non-disputants such as Japan, Russia, Australia, South Korea, and India also contributed to a heightened sense of combativeness in the region. Another explanation for the militarization that is not observed in MLE operations but nonetheless remains notable in the region is China’s growing capability as a military technology developer and producer. It is the sole political entity among the disputants to have achieved a high level of military development and expansion in the past decade.
Second, disputant parties are found to be inclined to react to China’s military and administrative build-up by taking similar but belated actions as well as to take legal actions to constrain other parties, rather than to establish a sustainable legal order in the maritime domain. Perhaps the fact that disputants are prioritizing their political interests, i.e., protecting one’s territorial claims and rights through the law, should not be surprising, because after all the waters, islands, and resources in the South China Sea carry both economic and geopolitical significance. Pending a collaborative mechanism among disputing parties in the tourism sector, even if the race to promote tourism services in disputed islands is unlikely to lead to a tourism war, it would be overly optimistic to assume that such a race would not exacerbate the geopolitical tensions in the South China Sea. The build-up of administrative and military infrastructure in the disputed features is already destabilizing the region.
Going forward, the study suggests more attention be directed to issues that promote shared interests such as joint fishery and marine resource management. Such work could build on Borton [8] regarding the need to collaborate among South China Sea disputants in the conservation of marine biodiversity and fisheries sustainability. Based on his field research, Borton [8] not only highlights the depletion of fishery resources and declines in marine and coastal ecosystems but also calls for increased cooperation in this regard among stakeholders, including marine scientists, diplomats, maritime law enforcement bodies, families and fishers, etc. In addition to promoting shared interests in marine conservation, the study calls for increasing dialogues as well as joint maritime exercises not just among existing allies and partners but also among other relevant parties. In the coming years, as disputants continue their quiet construction and expansion of artificial islands, the presence of trawlers, marine vessels, and coast guard vessels in the overlapping waters may be more regularized. As all parties would benefit from reduced frictions and unwanted maritime incidents, current dialogues, negotiations, and maritime exercises relating to the South China Sea should address specific measures to better handle unplanned encounters at sea involving naval and non-naval vessel as well as ways to reduce illegal fishing and protect fishermen.

1	Economic	To track events pertaining to monetary, financial, and economic activities in the South China Sea.	This includes actions such as oil and gas exploration and exploitation, fishing agreements, trade negotiations, and economic partnerships between countries related to the South China Sea. The focus here is on activities that have a direct impact on the economic well-being of the involved nations and their economies. Events like resource exploitation, joint development projects, and economic cooperation fall under this category.
2	Administrative	To track actions related to the governance and management of the South China Sea region	This involves the establishment of regulations, policies, and legal frameworks that guide the use of the sea and its resources. Administrative events can include the delineation of maritime boundaries, the creation of protected areas, the implementation of environmental protection measures, and the enforcement of maritime laws. This category covers events that aim to regulate and maintain order in the maritime domain but on an administrative level.
3	Diplomatic	To track diplomatic talks, negotiations, and agreements aimed at resolving disputes, promoting cooperation, and fostering peaceful relations.	Diplomatic events can range from bilateral discussions to multilateral dialogues involving non-disputants, regional organizations or international forums. Examples include regional meetings, negotiations over territorial claims, and efforts to establish codes of conduct to prevent conflicts.
4	Military	To track actions directly involving the deployment of military forces or the use of military assets in the South China Sea.	Examples include military exercises, naval patrols, arms acquisitions, and the construction of military installations on islands and reefs.
5	Non-military/ maritime	To track events belonging to none of the above categories and concern developments of non-military maritime nature in the area.	It includes actions related to scientific research, environmental conservation, humanitarian efforts, cultural exchanges, and other non-military maritime activities. For instance, joint scientific research missions, disaster relief operations, and cultural exchanges between countries in the region would be categorized under this variable. The focus here is on events that contribute to collaboration and understanding rather than asserting military or economic dominance.
Data availability
The data is a work in progress and expected to be published upon the acquisition of a proper research grant.
