China has sunk deep economic roots into South-east Asia. Last year, Asean-China trade totalled US$878.2 billion (S$1.2 trillion), more than 22 times the value of trade at the turn of the century. While the United States invests more in the region, Beijing’s economic links are far stronger. Trade data paints a picture of the state of play in the region.


Singapore is South-east Asia’s trade and economic hub, and the island nation has free trade agreements with both the US and China. Its free trade agreements have made it an attractive hub for US and Chinese firms expanding into the region.


Bilateral trade between Indonesia, Asean’s biggest economy, and China came up to US$124.4 billion in 2021, more than three times its trade with the US (US$36.5 billion). Beijing has been Jakarta’s largest trading partner since 2013.


Malaysia is one of the top 10 exporters of liquefied natural gas to China. Chinese customs data showed that Kuala Lumpur exported more than US$25 billion worth of mineral products to China in 2021. This includes fossil fuels such as natural gas and petroleum.


Vietnam’s two-way trade with the US is the highest among Asean nations at US$112.9 billion. Some US companies have moved manufacturing and supply chains from China to the country because of the US-China trade war and Covid-19 pandemic.


Durians are one of Thailand’s key exports to China - more than 875,000 tonnes were shipped in 2021. Thailand’s agricultural department says this generated US$3.4 billion, more than half the US$6.5 billion of Thai fruits and nuts imported by China last year.

The US is Asean’s top investor

It accounts for about a quarter of Asean’s total foreign direct investment (FDI) in 2020. But these investments are concentrated in a handful of Asean states, such as Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand and Indonesia. In Cambodia and Laos, FDI from China has constituted the largest source of investment for consecutive years.

The US and China are locked in a battle for influence over South-east Asia, home to more than 660 million people and the fifth-largest economy in the world.
But when it comes to economics and trade, America has some catching up to do.
Over the years, China has cultivated considerable economic links with Asean, which sits on its doorstep and covers key trade routes. China has leapfrogged Washington’s economic dominance in the region.

A race to the cloud in Asean
How deep is China’s trade foothold in the region?
In terms of goods, Beijing is the top trading partner of all Asean countries, except Laos and Brunei.
China’s massive market of 1.4 billion people is a sponge, soaking up imports from the region on everything ranging from liquefied natural gas to electronics.
Asean’s 660 million-strong market is a major destination for Chinese goods, particularly with a middle class that is projected to grow to make up two-thirds of the region’s population by 2030.
Dr Sarah Tong, senior research fellow at the National University of Singapore’s East Asian Institute, said Beijing has “considerably deepened” its economic ties with South-east Asia since the early 2000s.
Trade between Asean and China has more than doubled in the past decade and almost quadrupled since the Asean-China Trade in Goods Agreement came into force in 2005.

Dr Tong said: “In recent years, as China faces growing domestic and external challenges to sustain future development, deepening economic relations with regional partners has become even more important.”

Where do America and China stand when it comes to investment?
Investment is where America has the advantage for now.
The US is Asean’s top investor, with its foreign direct investment (FDI) stock in Asean at US$328.5 billion in 2020. American firms invested US$35 billion into Asean in 2020, compared with China's US$7.7 billion that same year, according to the Asean Stats data portal.
America's direct investment in Asean is led by non-bank institutions that provide financial services. The US also invests heavily in manufacturing and wholesale trade in Asean.

Part of these trade and investment disparities arise from differences between the US and Chinese economies, said American Enterprise Institute (AEI) senior fellow Zack Cooper.
For instance, he said, exporting to South-east Asia can be difficult for many American sectors because of the long distances involved. US products in some categories are going to be expensive for Asean compared with Chinese goods.
Said Dr Cooper: “So much of the US economy is really built around finance and investing. I think it's natural for American investment to be a far larger portion of US activity in the region than it would be for China.”
Moreover, the investment gap between the US and China isn’t quite as wide if FDI figures from the mainland are combined with Hong Kong’s. In 2020, this amounted to about US$28 billion, according to figures from Beijing and the Hong Kong Trade Development Council.
And in some Asean member states such as Cambodia and Laos, FDI from China has constituted the largest source of investment for consecutive years, according to data from the Asean Investment Report last September by the Asean Secretariat and United Nations Conference on Trade and Development.
The same report noted that investment from China has been rising steadily, from an annual average of $6.9 billion in the 2011 to 2015 period, to an average of $11.5 billion per year from 2016 to 2020 - an increase of about 67 per cent.

Chinese investment in key sectors such as construction and IT services is growing rapidly, increasing by more than 250 per cent in 2020 from the year before to reach US$1.6 billion and US$656 million respectively, according to figures from Beijing.
Experts say this suggests China’s investments are targeted at what the region needs - towards infrastructure in developing nations, and IT services like data centres.

Under the Belt and Road Initiative, Beijing is building mega infrastructure projects, such as ports and high-speed railways, in Asean nations - something the US simply will not match.
Said AEI’s Dr Cooper: “There's a fundamental asymmetry here, which is that the US doesn't determine what its private sector does.
Unlike in China, where the government has more involvement in and leverage over Chinese businesses, America’s private sector is largely driven by assessments of expected returns on investment and risk profiles, he said. The most that any US administration can do is roll out incentives to encourage companies to invest somewhere.

“The US government is never going to be able to announce billions and billions of dollars in new US government-driven investment in South-east Asia, just because that's not how our system works. But that doesn't mean that every day, there aren’t hundreds of US companies looking to invest in the region,” said Dr Cooper.
Stimson Centre senior fellow Yun Sun, too, noted that China’s state-dominated approach to trade and investment is not a model that the US can offer.
“For avid South-east Asian observers, this might be a reality they have to reckon with. The US and China each have their own comparative advantage in economic engagement and broader relations with the region,” she said.
While China is focused on economics, the US has been more focused on security. Washington wants to change that.
The issue, as many analysts see it, is that Washington’s engagement of the Indo-Pacific region has been primarily focused on security, while Beijing is addressing bread-and-butter issues of trade and economics.
Under the Biden administration, Washington has strengthened partnerships to confront China, which includes the Five Eyes (Australia, Britain, Canada, New Zealand and the US) intelligence-sharing alliance, Quad (Quadrilateral Security Dialogue comprising Australia, India, Japan and the US) and trilateral Aukus (Australia-United Kingdom-US).
The US military also regularly conducts freedom of navigation operations in the South China Sea, and military exercises with countries including the Philippines.
Washington has grown increasingly worried about losing economic ground to China in South-east Asia, and successive US administrations have spoken about America’s desire to deepen its engagement with the region.
But America still lacks a multilateral trade strategy in the region, and there is little appetite in Washington for free trade deals today.

Experts say trade arrangements will continue to propel the growth of trade between Asean and China, while America’s lack of involvement in the region’s multilateral deals will get in the way of deepening its economic ties with Asean.
In January 2017, the US bowed out of the Trans-Pacific Partnership, which later became the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP).
In contrast, China is part of the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP) which came into effect this year. Besides China, the trade pact includes 10 Asean states, Japan, South Korea, Australia and New Zealand - and it is estimated to boost global trade by almost US$500 billion annually by 2030.
Beijing has also formally applied to join the CPTPP, although it is unlikely to be granted access so soon.
Will the Indo-Pacific Economic Framework help the US to catch up?
The Biden administration is scheduled to launch its Indo-Pacific Economic Framework (IPEF) on May 23. But officials have been clear that this economic plan will not be a traditional free trade deal with market access, which is arguably at the top of South-east Asian wishlists.

Instead, IPEF will focus on four “pillars” of supply chain resilience, infrastructure and clean energy, anti-corruption, and fair and resilient trade, which includes the digital economy and labour standards.
“For most countries in South-east Asia, the Indo-Pacific Economic Framework will be seen as a poor consolation prize in place of what they really want: concrete market access commitments from the US,” said Hinrich Foundation senior research fellow Stephen Olson.
“The agenda appears to be far more reflective of what the US wants rather than what Asean wants.”
The Stimson Centre’s Ms Sun said that while the IPEF’s four pillars may be appealing from the American narrative and perspective, countries in the region may find them difficult to take part in - for instance, the higher trade standards - or expensive, such as tackling corruption.
“The IPEF may not be what the less developed countries in the region find most needed for where they are,” she said.
Dr Tong also pointed out that while the framework echoes the CPTPP through its emphasis on fair and resilient trade, it was “unclear how appealing these ‘high-standard issues’ will be to prospective members of the IPEF, other than the developed members in the region, without better market access promises”.
Other analysts such as ISEAS - Yusof Ishak Institute’s Dr Choong say Washington may be able to cajole more developed Asean members to join the IPEF, but it will be a hard sell to less developed states such as Cambodia or Laos.

“These countries want the meat and potatoes, and when it comes to free trade agreements, this means access to the US market and lower tariffs. The TPP was supposed to be it, but without this market access, it's difficult for Washington to bring something substantive to the table,” he said.
Depending on the details, however, Asean members are likely to find some aspects desirable, said Mr Olson.
For instance, if the digital trade provisions can help small and medium-sized enterprises access global markets, that could be quite beneficial to a region dominated by such companies, he said.
However, if other provisions prohibit local data storage requirements or require open cross-border data flows, at least some governments in the region would have a hard time signing on, Mr Olson added.
Asia Society Policy Institute vice-president Wendy Cutler, a former acting deputy US Trade Representative, acknowledged that some in the region would undoubtedly be disappointed that the IPEF will not be a traditional trade agreement with market access offers, at least for the time being.
“But it’s important that potential candidate economies closely examine the various pillars of IPEF to assess what benefits and incentives beyond market access will be provided to make the initiative attractive for them,” she said.
The focus on supply chain resilience, for instance, could be beneficial because supply chain disruptions are leading many businesses to reconsider their sourcing patterns and destinations for new investments, she said.
“With a dedicated pillar to resilient supply chains, the IPEF can go a long way in helping countries and businesses rethink and restructure their supply chains,” she added.

Singapore Prime Minister Lee Hsien Loong said: “We encourage greater Asean participation in the IPEF and we hope the US will directly invite and engage Asean member states in this endeavour.
There remains a strong demand for US engagement in the region, fuelled in part by a wariness towards China’s growing economic might.
The ISEAS survey showed nearly two-thirds of respondents were concerned about Beijing’s growing economic clout.
Mr Olson said: “While China’s economic engagement has been beneficial and welcomed in most cases, there is also some trepidation about China’s ability to use its economic might to bully, or otherwise exploit, its partners.
“US engagement in the region, especially economically, is desired by many in Asean, if for no other reason than to counterbalance China’s rising influence,” he added.
And there is also hope that the IPEF can be a stepping stone that draws the US into deeper engagement in the region, which could, in time, turn out to be more reflective of Asean’s interests, he added.
