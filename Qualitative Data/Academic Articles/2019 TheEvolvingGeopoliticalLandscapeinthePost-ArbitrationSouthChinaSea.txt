The Evolving Geopolitical Landscape in the PostArbitration South China Sea
Serafettin Yilmaz (Shifan Yao) and Fu-Kuo Liu

This research offers a policy analysis of the post-Arbitration geopolitics in
the South China Sea (SCS). It aims, ﬁrst, to lay out the particular internal
and external factors that have led to noticeable changes in the Philippines
and Vietnam’s SCS policies, and, second, to explore the implications of the
ongoing shift in the regional politics for the East Asian strategic landscape.
In this regard, two distinct tendencies with respect to geopolitical transformations are highlighted: while some actors are observed to put more
emphasis on security and seek strategic diversiﬁcation, others appear to
put more emphasis on development and seek economic diversiﬁcation.
Key words: South China Sea, China, The Philippines, Vietnam, regional
geopolitics.

Introduction
The South China Sea (SCS) is a strategic waterway, connecting one of the
most developed manufacturing centers of the world in East Asia with the world’s
largest hydrocarbon producing regions in the Middle East. It is home to vital
maritime trade routes connecting the Paciﬁc and Indian Oceans, commanding
more than half of the global merchant ﬂeet tonnage. The region also contains
substantial oil and gas reserves, precious materials, rich ﬁsheries and other
sources of marine protein. Consequently, the question of safety and security in
the SCS is of paramount signiﬁcance for economic development in the Asia
Paciﬁc.1
Over the past few decades, the geopolitical landscape in the SCS has been
deﬁned by persistent territorial disputes among six major claimants. The disputes
are rather varied, including overlapping claims of sovereignty over land features

Arbitration granted Manila a victory as, on 12 July 2016, the SCS arbitral tribunal
ruled mostly in favor of the Philippines, including submissions on historic rights and
the Nine-Dash Line, status of features, and lawfulness of Chinese actions in the SCS.7

China responded to the SCS Arbitration Award by reiterating its initial
position,8 indicating that the tribunal established unilaterally upon the request by
the Philippines was neither an international court nor a permanent court for arbitration and had no jurisdiction over sovereignty disputes between states. The Filipino government, on the other hand, sought to rally support and build up
pressure on Beijing. In the region, it tried to elicit support from ASEAN, other
inter-regional groupings such as the Asia–Europe Meeting (ASEM), and from
non-claimant actors such as Japan and Australia. Internationally, the Aquino
Administration worked to ensure recognition from the United States and the
European Union (EU), receiving, in turn, vocal support from several regional
and international players.
In this research, we offer an analysis of the post-Arbitration geopolitics in the
South China Sea. We observe that ever since the release of the Award by the arbitral tribunal, regional situation has considerably evolved with each major actor
recalibrating their strategy as it relates to the geopolitics in the SCS. In this respect,
we take particular note of the Philippines and Vietnam, the two major stakeholders
in the maritime territorial disputes, and ﬁnd that regional politics indicates an inclination toward a new policy discourse that, on the one hand, suggests strategic diversiﬁcation through which the parties aim to expand their strategic options to ensure
better regional and international environment and, on the other, economic diversiﬁcation through which the actors seek to reinforce their connectivity and external
market outreach for healthier and more sustainable economic development. Hence,
the purpose of this study is two-pronged: ﬁrst, it lays out the particular internal and
external factors that have led to certain structural changes in the Philippines and
Vietnam’s regional diplomacy; second, it analyzes the prospects and potential implications of the ongoing shifts in the SCS for the East Asian strategic landscape.

Post-Arbitration Regional Geopolitics: International Law and
Geopolitics
Historically, there has been contention over the validity of international law as
a law given the fact that its applicability rests upon the acceptance by the sovereign states, which, theoretically, international law is designed to govern. It is held
that the emergence of international relations theory, as a branch of science distinct from international law in the early post-War era, was in part due to the
apparent contradiction between what was considered ideal and what was real.

Drawing on this basic framework, we put forward two primary observations.
First, we maintain that the new post-Arbitration status quo in the SCS invokes a
particular situation, albeit not without precedents, in which geopolitics eventually
overrides international law.9 Second, we ﬁnd that, in the speciﬁc Arbitration case
of Philippines v. China, although the tribunal decision initially appeared to throw
the region into a deeper state of chaos, under the force of a number of regional
and extra-regional developments, intra-regional relations have taken a more amicable turn for better economic and political communication.
For the students of the realist school, such a shift is perhaps anticipated.10
Scholars such as Edward Hallett Carr and Hans Morgenthau long suggested that
the realm of international law did not involve idealism but power politics in
which the state remained to be the ultimate decision maker.11 Certainly, since the
end of the Cold War, international law and judicial institutions have proliferated
and begun to be involved in a much broader aspect of international life in an
effort to ensure equal treatment of states, large and small. Yet, they still remain
subject to the states’ willingness (and capacity) to participate and comply with
them, thereby failing to exert absolute authority over and “constrain a powerful
state on its own.”12 At times, international law itself may be inﬂuenced by dominant state actors which intend to utilize global legal institutions instrumentally
for certain ends.13
Thus, when compliance is not ensured due to the lack of an enforcement
structure, or a fair outcome cannot be guaranteed under hegemonic intervention,
legitimacy and effectiveness of legal rules and regimes become questionable.

To be sure, states acknowledge and observe the established principles of international law more often than they choose not to recognize or abide.14 Yet, however sporadic, the occurrence of non-recognition or non-compliance suggests
that the question of applicability of certain legal regimes still remains. Beijing’s
critique toward the formation procedure of the arbitral tribunal and the clandestine involvement of third parties in making a ruling on the case, therefore, heralds an instance of non-compliance and the overcoming of an international
convention by more urgent geopolitical realities.15
The SCS Arbitration casts an interesting light over the nature of interaction
between international politics (power) and international law (norms) in which
neither (state) interests nor capabilities match adequately – the preconditions for
international law to exist.16 Thus, the apparent incompatibility between the two
and the disadvantaged status of the latter has, perhaps inadvertently, led to signiﬁcant changes in the foreign policy strategies of regional actors, more speciﬁcally, the Philippines and Vietnam. The readjustments involve the two
countries’ conceptualization of power politics in the Asia Paciﬁc, the structure
of their relations with major states, and the formulation of their regional diplomatic discourse as it relates to economics and politics. To be sure, initially, both
Manila and, in a more implicit way, Hanoi, put faith in a rearranged postArbitration regional structure under the force of the Arbitration. Nevertheless, an
even greater faith on high politics that could forcefully reshape regional order
more to their favor still continued to override the particular trust on the inherent
viability of international law and conventions. Essentially, the two countries
viewed the UNCLOS more as a means than a standard by itself.17 For that reason, both Manila and Hanoi sought intervention by the United States and its
allies on their behalf.

The Philippines
A former US colony, the Philippines’ relations with the United States has long
been the cornerstone of the country’s security strategy. Cooperation between the
two countries dates back to 1947 when, one year after the US granted independence, Manila signed a defense agreement (which was terminated in 1992 and
replaced by Visiting Forces Agreement in 1998), allowing the United States to
establish military bases in multiple locations across the country (used extensively
by the United States during the Vietnam War) and assist in the establishment and
training of the Armed Forces of the Philippines. In 1951, the two sides signed a
mutual defense treaty which was later upgraded under Enhanced Defense Cooperation Agreement (EDCA) of 2014 with an initial term of 10 years. Although
the EDCA did not authorize the establishment of permanent US bases, it nonetheless reinforced US military presence in the country.
The worsening situation in the SCS, and the Aquino government’s intention
“to constrain China” under the framework of the Obama administration’s
Rebalance to the Asia-Paciﬁc strategy, provided the required justiﬁcation for an
increase in military expenditure and the signing of the agreement, which allowed
“the most extensive access to Philippine military facilities since… the early
1990s.”18 The contentious situation reached a climax when the government of
Benigno Aquino III requested an arbitral tribunal at the Permanent Court of Arbitration (PCA) to consider China’s SCS maritime claims in February 2013. During the Arbitration, the US government backed Manila verbally, characterizing
the move as a “step in the right direction.”19 The Philippines, for its part, strived

aftermath of the Arbitration, China’s effective non-recognition of the result of
the Award, the questions regarding the formation and process of the tribunal
(which created legitimacy issues), and Manila’s tactical inclusion into its Submissions of the Taiping Island (Itu Aba) – the largest naturally-formed land feature

uncertainties and undermined the prospects of viable gains. Furthermore, the

further manifested. For example, ASEAN has failed to weigh in as a single, uniﬁed body and draw a clear roadmap that would convince all the stakeholders to
follow.67 Finally, the United States, which is not a party to the UNCLOS, has

Conclusion
This study ﬁnds that the Southeast Asian regional geopolitical landscape has
changed considerably in the post-Arbitration period. Unlike the early predictions,
the Philippines’ victory at the tribunal has not fundamentally transformed the
regional order into a more contentious form. In fact, it has generated an opposite
outcome, acting as a catalyzer for a new type of regional geopolitics. While the
disputes that have deﬁned the security landscape over the past few decades still
exist, much less emphasis is placed on them by their direct stakeholders. Both
external (such as the lack of resolute action by the United States to force China
into a change of behavior following the tribunal ruling and the decision to withdraw from the TPP) and internal factors (such as the change of leadership in the
Philippines and Vietnam, and the emergence of China-led institutions under the
Belt and Road framework) have been decisive in this transformation.
Indeed, recently, regional politics has transformed visibly under more pragmatist leaderships that view sustainable economic growth as an extension of their
overall national security. The Rebalance to the Asia-Paciﬁc strategy has lost
much of its viability as the Trump administration has rejected it and a new discourse has yet to take its place.81 In this respect, although the “open and free
Indo-Paciﬁc” mentioned in the US National Security Strategy (2017) (and a more
recent announcement of a $113m investment in the region) indicates a possible
course for a new strategy, what sort of a shape it will eventually morph into and
the plan’s overall viability is still questionable.

That said, certain factors that may lead to new volatilities in the SCS still exist.
First of all, although the United States has apparently lost a certain amount of
credibility, overall it is still a dominant external actor. The recent developments
regarding Terminal High Altitude Area Defense (THAAD) deployments in South
Korea and Japan, and arms sales to the island of Taiwan, clearly indicate that the
change of regional discourse from one of securitization to economic development
is limited at best and the trend might quickly be reversed. Second, whereas countries such as the Philippines and Malaysia began to establish new security and
economic linkages with China, other stakeholders such as Vietnam and Singapore appear to move closer to the United States and form broader security
arrangements. In this regard, especially Vietnam continues to seek to internationalize the SCS issue. Third, the development-oriented discourse led by Duterte
has yet to translate into a more solid action in the SCS, although certain progress
has been made.82 Indeed, if the domestic and/or regional (geo)political conditions
shift, the country may once again recalibrate its SCS strategy, seeking to, among
others, “invoke the arbitration award as a basis to encourage and justify more
expansive and multilateral Freedom of Navigation Operations by the United
States, Japan, and other likeminded countries….”83 Thus, current cooperation
efforts may be short-lived, as has been the case in numerous instances in the past
in which various joint development efforts eventually failed.84
In the ﬁnal analysis, the overall picture in the SCS invokes a situation in which
international relations prevail over international law due largely to the complexity
of regional disputes among sovereign actors and the reality of realpolitik in
which power imbalances and interest discrepancies exist.85 Hence, regional geopolitics continue to be ﬂuid and highly conditional. The apparent lack of a practical solution to the crisis prompts the related countries to seek alternative courses
of action. In Southeast Asia, the direction appears to be from international frameworks, such as arbitral tribunal, to regional settings and dialogue mechanisms,
such as conﬁdence building measures and code of conduct. The new framework
is further reinforced by what seems to be a stronger political will to ensure comprehensive economic development, which requires opening up regional communication and integration channels, and sidelining disputes and disagreements.
