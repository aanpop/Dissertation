executive summary
This article assesses the role of maritime law-enforcement (MLE) vessels in
an apparent trend of heightened competition in the South China Sea and
identifies patterns in the maritime policing by MLE vessels of the disputing
South China Sea claimants during unplanned encounters at sea.

main argument
The South China Sea disputes, involving six political entities—China, Taiwan,
Vietnam, Malaysia, Brunei, and the Philippines—have been a political flashpoint
in Southeast Asia since the 1970s. The emergence and growing prominence of
civilian MLE vessels (conventionally known as marine police or coast guard
vessels) suggests that disputants are taking new measures to assert their claims
without overtly militarizing them. Maritime policing has increased in frequency
and intensity in the past decade. Yet, when considering four factors—the use of
forcible measures, physical damage, human injuries/casualties, and diplomatic
protests—an analysis of publicly reported naval incidents in the South China
Sea between 2010 and 2022 suggests that conflict escalation primarily happens
at sea between the crews. Such incidents, which range from single-day events
to months-long confrontations, have, in the worst cases, resulted in intense
diplomatic standoffs lasting longer than two months. In general, however,
disputants have refrained from taking hostile political or legal actions against
harassment at sea.

policy implications
• Given that reducing misunderstanding and mitigating the risks of physical
confrontations is of benefit to all the South China Sea disputants, ongoing
talks for a code of conduct for the sea should include a proper legal
framework for MLE operations. This framework—to be separate from
the existing Code for Unplanned Encounters at Sea, which only applies to
naval vessels—should stipulate the threat and/or use of force at sea by MLE
vessels because coast guard enforcement duties may require different levels
of force.
• Disputants should re-establish and refocus talks to address joint fisheries
and marine resource management in disputed waters. They would also
benefit from reinforcing the regional hotline for maritime emergencies and
setting up a crisis management hotline at the coast guard level.
• Regional MLE agencies should increase communications both onshore
and offshore and hold joint exercises and exchanges that would increase
mutual understanding and trust, which would subsequently help prevent
unexpected consequences.

T

he South China Sea, the largest semi-enclosed sea in the world,
has been a political flashpoint in Southeast Asia since the 1970s.1
Six political entities—China, Taiwan, Vietnam, Malaysia, Brunei, and the
Philippines—have competing claims over some or all of the archipelagic
structures in the sea, including the Spratly and Paracel Islands, islets,
and rocks.2 While some disputed areas in the sea have never been fully
explored and may be uninhabitable, the Spratly Island group is of particular
contestation, with Vietnam, China, and Taiwan claiming it in its entirety
and the Philippines, Brunei, and Malaysia claiming parts of it. Based on its
so-called nine-dash-line map, China has made a claim to the South China Sea
that covers nearly the sea’s entirety, and it has occupied the Paracel Islands since
1974, despite competing claims by Taiwan and Vietnam. And while Indonesia
does not claim territory in the South China Sea, it can also be considered a
disputant in maritime conflicts because its exclusive economic zone (EEZ)
overlaps with China’s nine-dash-line claim. Given that Indonesia and China
frequently clash over fishing rights near Indonesia’s Natuna Islands, Indonesia
is pertinent to activities in and decisions about the sea.
Studies have shown that over the past decades parties to the disputes have
adopted and adapted different tactics, whether military, administrative, or
diplomatic, to assert and solidify their South China Sea claims.3 One increasingly
prominent tactic has been the dispatch of maritime law-enforcement (MLE)
vessels to patrol and exercise maritime police authority in their own EEZs and
the contested waters. While many MLE agencies within the member states of
the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) are now under civilian
jurisdiction (see Appendix 1), the operational spectrum of these organizations
still involves not only civil defense but also different levels of combat duties. In
the absence of a manual or uniform framework for MLE operations, there is

and the difficulty of managing tensions in multiparty disputes over territorial
issues, this article explores the operations of MLE vessels from both legal
and practical perspectives in the South China Sea. In particular, the article
identifies the patterns of maritime policing by MLE vessels of dispute
claimants during unplanned encounters at sea. On that basis, the article draws
insights regarding MLE operations in the management of territorial disputes.
This research provides an updated analysis on the role of MLE in what
appears to be a competing, rather than cooperative, trend in the regional
maritime domain. Until now, the only dataset on South China Sea incidents
is one that has been compiled by the China Power Project at the Center for
Strategic and International Studies (CSIS).5 But given that the CSIS dataset
has not been updated since early 2020 and that the data coding leaves many
questions unanswered, the author has re-collected, verified, and recoded the
raw data on these maritime incidents. This research yields a new, up-to-date, and
more suitable dataset on MLE activities in the South China Sea. In particular,
the revised dataset provides information, where available, on the presence of

maritime policing: theory and practice
MLE vessels, more conventionally referred to as marine police or coast
guard vessels, are often operated by a civilian government agency that is
separate from, though likely coordinates with, naval vessels for the purpose
of patrolling, policing, and protecting fisheries and other marine resources.7
While there is a growing body of research into the development of MLEs
in the Asia-Pacific region, with considerable focus on Southeast Asia,8 the
linkage between MLE operations and the management of disputes, not least
in the South China Sea, remains debatable. On the one hand, as civilian
or paramilitary organizations, MLE bodies, at an institutional level, have
the potential to facilitate regional maritime cooperation and confidence
building.9 On the other hand, MLE vessels are being increasingly deployed
not just as a tool to police territorial waters but also as an instrument of
foreign policy in waters beyond the limits of national jurisdiction. Given that
an increasing use of force has been noted in many maritime encounters,10

Legal Authorizations
The exercise of law-enforcement powers at sea by states against foreign
vessels is premised on states’ territorial jurisdiction as well as a set of
principles in customary international law, including the 1982 United Nations
Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS). Examples include the principle
of self-defense, which is considered an exception to the prohibition against the
use of force under Article 2(4) of the UN Charter and customary international
law, and the sovereign immunity granted to foreign warships and other
government vessels operated for noncommercial purposes under Articles 32,
95, 96, and 236 of UNCLOS.11 However, in addition to sovereign rights over
a coastal state’s territorial sea, which extends 12 nautical miles (nm) from the
baseline, UNCLOS also grants a coastal state two additional sovereign rights:
the right to natural resources in its EEZ, which extends up to a maximum of
200 nm from the coastal baseline, and the right to exploitation of resources in
the seabed and subsoil of its continental shelf, which can extend seaward up
to a maximum of 350 nm from the baseline.12
Thus, UNCLOS grants coastal states and foreign states different rights
and duties in different maritime zones (see Figure 1). In particular, the
coastal state has complete and exclusive sovereign rights in its internal waters,
including its territorial sea. These rights encompass the jurisdiction under
Article 28 to arrest or investigate on board a foreign ship that has infringed on
local laws while lying in or passing through the territorial sea.13 Beyond the
territorial sea, Article 27 of UNCLOS authorizes the coastal state to exercise
criminal jurisdiction on board a foreign vessel only if (1) the consequences of
the crime extend to the coastal state, (2) the crime disturbs the peace or order
of the coastal state and the territorial sea, (3) the shipmaster, a diplomatic
agent, or a consular official of the flag state has asked for assistance from local
authorities, or (4) action is needed to intercept illicit drug traffic.14
What can be seen is that the extended maritime zones require coastal
states to build the appropriate institutions to exercise their power and rights

In sum, Articles 27, 28, 73, 105, 110, and 111 of UNCLOS lay out the
jurisdiction of coastal states concerning foreign vessels.15 On this basis, the
legal authority framework for MLE can be summed up in eight types of actions:
(1) patrolling, (2) signaling, stopping, blocking, restricting the maneuvering
space of another vessel, or boarding the suspect vessel, (3) searching the suspect
vessel or the people aboard, (4) detaining or arresting people aboard the
suspect vessel and/or the suspect vessel itself, (5) seizing items on the suspect
vessel, (6) escorting, directing, or steaming the suspect vessel, with its people
and cargo, to a state port or similar place for investigation, (7) conducting an
investigation, and (8) subsequently prosecuting or imposing administrative
action or sanction.16 A typical example of maritime patrolling and policing is
the interception of a fishing vessel for either alleged illegal fishing or fishing
in a disputed zone.
While the legal bases for MLE actions are generally agreed upon, as
institutionalized in several countries’ coast guard acts and regulations, there
remain significant hurdles to identifying the appropriate maritime policing
authority and distinguishing the suitable use of force at sea. The issue is
further complicated by the fact that several South China Sea disputants
have issued regulations or laws that effectively authorize MLE forces to use
arms in the hot pursuit of a foreign vessel encroaching on the disputant’s
territorial sea and leaving toward the high seas. For instance, in 2015,
Vietnam enacted a decree that authorizes the use of “tool and weapon in line
with the law” in exercising the right of hot pursuit, though it did not specify
which weapons the law allows.17 Similarly, there have been concerns about
the use of force under China’s new Maritime Police Law, which took effect
on February 1, 2022, as maritime police agencies are given the right to take
“all necessary measures, including the use of weapons” and “to take measures
such as forced eviction” to stop foreign vessels or individuals from infringing
on China’s territorial sovereignty, sovereign rights, or jurisdiction.18 While
the use of weapons in maritime policing, especially during hot pursuit,

Law-Enforcement Practices in the South China Sea
In practice, maritime policing in the South China Sea faces
treacherous currents, not just because of overlapping jurisdictions in the
disputed waters but for several other reasons. First, there are insufficient
cooperative mechanisms among regional coast guards (Table 1)—and the
mechanisms that currently exist are inadequate because they are either
too narrow in scope or not effective enough. For instance, although the
dispute claimants are all parties to the Regional Cooperation Agreement
on Combating Piracy and Armed Robbery against Ships in Asia (ReCAAP),
this multilateral agreement focuses mainly on promoting and enhancing
government-to-government cooperation against piracy and armed
robbery of ships in Asia. Launched in November 2006, ReCAAP now has
21 members across four continents and runs the Information Sharing Centre
in Singapore that encourages member coast guards to share information
and engage in capacity building for combating piracy and armed robbery.
In intra-ASEAN cooperation, two cooperative mechanisms stand out, yet
neither is adequately inclusive: the Malacca Strait Patrol initiative, which
involves Singapore, Indonesia, Malaysia, and Thailand; and the trilateral
maritime patrol arrangement in the Sulu and the Celebes Seas through
the Trilateral Cooperative Arrangement that involves Indonesia, Malaysia,
and the Philippines. Since 2015, the ASEAN Coast Guard Forum has also

Fourth, the less aggressive nature of MLE vessels does not necessarily

materials and method
Data Collection
The construction of the dataset on maritime incidents in the South China

Of the 115 incidents recorded for this study, China was the instigator
in 85 incidents, or 73.9%. Vietnam and the Philippines were the two biggest
opposing parties, raising their opposition in 57 and 28 incidents (49.6% and
24.3% of the total), respectively.
The high number of clashes recorded in 2014 is due to the oil rig
standoff between Vietnam and China. The heated maritime confrontations
and diplomatic exchanges, which lasted from May 1 to July 15, started after
the China National Offshore Oil Corporation deployed its Haiyang Shiyou
981 oil rig for the first time in Vietnam-claimed waters.42 Vessel ramming
by both sides and months-long anti-China protests in Vietnam followed the
oil rig’s deployment.
In the overall timeline, there was an evident surge of incidents between
2014 and 2017 and a dramatic decline from 2018. There are three potential
explanations. First, the dataset relies on publicly available reports of maritime
incidents in English, Chinese, and Vietnamese. That means incidents reported
in local languages in Malaysia and Brunei might be underrepresented.
Second, as noted above, disputants will decide whether to publicize a
maritime incident, particularly one that involves China, depending on their
economic and political motives. There are reasons to believe that when
Vietnam or the Philippines wants to pressure China on disputes involving
the South China Sea, their respective governments may be more vocal about
maritime incidents. In a similar way, governments might also choose not to
report or make public certain incidents as a deliberate attempt to contain a
conflict behind closed doors. Malaysia, in particular, tends to keep out of
public view any maritime clashes with China, as well its concern regarding
Chinese coast guard vessels in Malaysia’s EEZ north of Borneo.43 Malaysia’s
reticence is likely due to the fact that China has been Malaysia’s top trading
partner for thirteen consecutive years.44 Third, claimants and interested
parties to the disputes may have developed new tactics to protect their
claims without resorting to physical confrontations and aggression. These
tactics include mobilizing maritime militias and deploying military assets on
artificial islands constructed on shoals and reefs in disputed parts of the sea.

Conflict Escalation at Sea
The empirical data points out a clear trend in conflict escalation at
sea. Figure 3 proposes a theoretical framework for understanding the
escalation of conflict intensity during unplanned maritime encounters. The
chain of events, as demonstrated in the dataset, involves (1) vessels coming
into contact in a disputed maritime zone, (2) confrontation, where verbal
exchanges and resistance ensue, (3) physical interference, whereby threats of
coercive measures and physical resistance follow, (4) stalemate, where the
physical standoff continues with either the arrival of additional vessels or the
refusal by one party to withdraw. This could be followed by either (5) the
use of forcible measures, where actions such as detention, arrest, or seizure of
assets are taken, with damages inflicted on physical assets and human beings,
or (6) disengagement, wherein all parties withdraw, flee, chase away, or are
released. Following either of these, (7) confrontation could occur, involving
exchanges of verbal and official diplomatic protests and rebuttals.
As discussed above, 85.2% of the reported incidents escalated to
level 3—physical interference—where the threat of forcible measures
was recorded (Table 4). Of these level 3 incidents, 74.5% escalated to
level 5, where the use of forcible measures was reported. Nonetheless, all
the incidents, regardless of the use of forcible measures, eventually ended
at level 6, disengagement. The most prolonged standoff, which was widely
reported, was the 2014 oil rig crisis between Vietnam and China, but that
ended with China’s withdrawal of the oil rig. Another equally long standoff
that was kept out of the public view was the one between Malaysia, China, and
Vietnam from December 2019 to February 2020. It also ended in restraint and

The intensification of conflict during unplanned encounters at sea also
illustrates the need to examine not just how MLE forces are run, whether they
are authorized to use force, and to what extent they might use force, but also what

for on-the-ground conflict management, which means that in virtually every
maritime incident, the exchange of verbal demands and resistance escalated
toward physical interference and resistance, and in more serious cases, the use of
forcible measures led to physical destruction and human injuries. Since settling
the disputes in the South China Sea among the multiple claimants is a difficult
and arduous process, it is vital to finalize a more binding code of conduct that
lays out the legal and practical frameworks for maritime policing in the area.

conclusion
This article provides empirical evidence on the handling of maritime
incidents among multiple claimants in the South China Sea over a twelve-year
period from 2010 to mid-2022. It also revisits the legal framework for maritime
policing and identifies patterns of conflict escalation and de-escalation in
reported unplanned encounters at sea. As a result of this discussion, the
article makes three recommendations to improve the operations of MLE and
defuse the possibilities of maritime conflict in the South China Sea.
First, the nature of MLE operations in the South China Sea, and the trends
seen in the data this article presents, suggest that a more comprehensive, legally
binding framework is needed to hold regional parties accountable. Such a
framework should address the jurisdictional complexity of MLE authorizations
and the management of maritime incidents involving MLE vessels. Given the
heightened risks for miscalculation and misunderstanding during unplanned
encounters at sea, it is beneficial to all parties to establish such a unified
maritime policing framework. This framework should be separate from CUES
(which only applies to naval vessels) and should address four major issues:
(1) the limits of threat and/or use of force at sea, including the use of lethal force
for self-defense versus the use of lethal force for MLE purposes, (2) the rights,
powers, and obligations for MLE officials and vessels in national laws and their
compatibility with regional guidelines, (3) the right of hot pursuit and cases
of hot pursuit toward or in a disputed maritime zone, and (4) humanitarian
assistance, human rights, and law enforcement at sea.

Second, a safety protocol for MLE in the South China Sea should be
accompanied by both the establishment of a joint fisheries management
scheme and the reinforcement of the regional crisis management hotline. The
findings from the data presented in this article shed light on how conflict
escalation in the maritime domain in the South China Sea has been largely
driven by participating vessels and individuals on the vessels rather than by
the governments of respective parties. Fishers and MLE agents frequently
come into contact because the vessels are stated to be fishing illegally, but
there has been no proper channel or protocol to address the issues of illegal
fishing or fishing in the disputed maritime zones. Although there are several
regional fisheries organizations and a few existing arrangements, these
provide technical support and capacity building rather than engage relevant
parties in specific ways to manage the activities of fisheries and the existence
of joint fisheries in a contested maritime area.51
Last, MLE agencies in the region should increase communications onshore
and offshore. More confidence-building measures, such as joint coast guard
exercises and exchanges, would increase mutual understanding, would build
greater relationships of trust, and could help prevent unforeseen encounters
from escalating into naval clashes or political problems. For instance, in
December 2022, for the first time, the Vietnam Coast Guard High Command
organized an exchange with coast guard forces of six other countries, namely
China, Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, Thailand, and Cambodia.52
Coincidentally, no maritime incidents were reported that month. Future
research should dive deeper into the formation and transformation of MLE
in response to emerging risks and threats at sea, as well as the connections
between maritime policing and maritime militias. Increased cooperation
and coordination among MLE forces could allow disputants to move beyond
the issues of maritime delimitation and work together productively in a
multilateral manner. 
