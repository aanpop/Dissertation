Abstract
How did the trade rivalry between the United States and China impact China’s foreign public
image in the ASEAN press? In the context of Southeast Asia’s mixed attitude toward China, this
article argues that the trade war between China and the United States heightened negative sentiment on China, especially in states grappling with the latter as a security threat, but whose
investment climate has been slower to respond to the restructuring of the global supply
chain. Drawing from econometric analyses of news sentiment mined from a corpus of
75,619 online English news articles in all 10 Southeast Asian countries from January 2016 to
March 2020, we ﬁnd that the trade war induced negative news sentiment on China in the
ASEAN press. However, foreign visits to and from China appear to have been favorably
regarded and helped boost China’s image in the region. The ﬁndings remind that there may
be a need for China to rethink its carrot-and-stick strategy to win the hearts and minds of
its ASEAN neighbors.
Keywords
ASEAN, China, news sentiment, online news, Southeast Asia, trade war

The image that neighbors hold about China varies, depending on historical enmities and on geopolitical developments in the last few years. Even in Southeast Asia, a region generally regarded as
“largely accommodating” of China (Kang, 2010: 132), citizens exhibit various degrees of reluctance, contingent on the extent of China’s contribution to domestic economy and politics (Cho
and Park, 2013). As recent domestic politics and trade policies pit China against the United
States, however, small states whose economies depend on both countries are forced to reexamine
ties in light of economic opportunities and security concerns.

How did the trade war between the United States and China contribute to current sentiment
on China in Southeast Asia? Did such perception have concrete implications for public diplomacy toward countries with economic and security linkages to both China and the United
States?
Even during campaign rallies way back in 2016, former US president Donald Trump had openly
accused China of unfair trade practices and of manipulating its currency to make its exports more
globally competitive (BBC News, 2016b). In 2018, Trump began implementing policies that set
tariffs and other trade barriers intended purportedly to correct the trade imbalance. China, in retaliation, imposed its own restrictive tariffs against US goods and even brought its case before the
World Trade Organization. Though the trade body subsequently ruled in China’s favor, declaring
US tariffs on Chinese tech goods “inconsistent” with international trade rules (BBC News, 2020b),
the decision did little to dissipate what seems to be a growing distrust of China. A 2013 Pew
Research Center report, for example, ﬁnds that of the 38 states surveyed, China is viewed favorably
by only 19—of which many are either recipients of substantial foreign aid or have become China’s
large commodity exporters (Pew Research Center, 2013). Meanwhile, in a report released in
October 2020, sentiment toward China is found to have grown more negative in recent years
across many advanced economies (Silver et al., 2020).
Some of China’s neighbors in Southeast Asia are likewise growing particularly worried about
China’s burgeoning military and economic clout and their possible implications for the region’s
security balance. In the Philippines, for instance, the 2013 Pew Report indicates that nine in 10
Filipinos regard China’s territorial encroachments as a big problem (Pew Research Center,
2013). A 2018 Social Weather Stations survey echoes these ﬁndings, showing that barely one in
four Filipinos were convinced that “most of what Chinese government wants to happen in the
Philippines is good for Filipinos” (SWS, 2019). In a 2019 Social Weather Stations Survey,
seven out of 10 adult Filipinos expressed worry about the growing number of Chinese workers
in the country due to relaxed licensing policies for Chinese offshore gaming operators. These offshore gambling operators have reportedly brought with them criminals and crime syndicates, and
have been linked to a number of illicit activities including forgery, kidnapping, money laundering,
and drug trafﬁcking (Robles, 2019).
Meanwhile in Indonesia, pre-existing anti-Chinese prejudice (Purdey, 2006), confounded by
the inﬂux of Chinese workers and fear that Chinese investments create a debt trap diplomacy,
seem to have worsened the already souring sentiment toward China (Tarahita and Rakhmat,
2019). A Pew Research report also found that in 2018 around four in 10 Indonesians viewed
China as a major national threat, a signiﬁcant jump from just 27 percent who believed so in
2013 (Tamir and Budiman, 2019). It is no different in Vietnam, where unfavorable opinion of
China is not only growing but becoming more inﬂamed. In 2018, for instance, the Vietnamese
government’s plan to open three special economic zones to long-term leases by Chinese companies was met by violent nationwide protests (Parameswaran, 2018). The event was reminiscent of
2014, when rioters stormed factories and left at least 21 people killed and 100 injured almost
immediately after China stationed an oil rig in waters claimed by Vietnam (Hodal and Kaiman,
2014).
Malaysia is perhaps an outlier for its public’s generally positive perception of China
(Pew Research Center, 2013), which observers attribute to Malaysian leaders’ pragmatic recognition of China’s economic and political signiﬁcance (Ahmad and Mohd Sani, 2017) and to
norms shaped by the Malaysian ethnic Chinese community’s emotional ties to the ancestral
homeland (Yeoh, 2019). But even so, Yeoh (2019: 412–413) clariﬁes that “there does not

Drawing from threat perception theory, this exploratory study posits that there is an economic
root to how citizens of ASEAN states perceived the US–China trade rivalry and such anxiety consequently mediated critical perception of China (Lerner et al., 2003). In the context of Southeast
Asia’s mixed attitude toward China, the trade war between China and the United States heightened
negative sentiment toward China, especially in member states contending with China’s territorial
encroachments but whose investment climate has been slower to respond to the restructuring of
the global supply chain. China’s assertiveness in the South China Sea and its growing inﬂuence
in the region raised concerns among ASEAN member states about the potential expansion of
China’s power at their expense. The trade war became intertwined with broader geopolitical
dynamics, amplifying the perception of China as a regional threat.
To empirically assess this relationship, we mined news stories on China which appeared in select
English language newspapers in all 10 Southeast Asian countries from January 2016 to March
2020. Analyzing sentiment in a corpus consisting of 75,619 news articles, we ﬁnd that, in
general, the trade war reinforced a critical opinion of China in the online press of Southeast
Asian economies grappling with growth uncertainty and disrupted markets. Interestingly, reports
of China’s encroachments on territorial waters claimed by some ASEAN member states do not
seem to have confounded a negative image, contrary to conventional expectation. Reports of
China’s coronavirus response, however, created a particularly negative sentiment, consistent
with standard accounts framing China as the pandemic’s epicenter. With respect to China’s
efforts toward public diplomacy, we ﬁnd that ofﬁcial statements related to cultural diplomacy
and those that appealed to Southeast Asia did not make a difference in selling China’s public
image in the region. What worked are high-level foreign visits to and from China, which seem
to be regarded quite favorably. The ﬁndings remind that China may perhaps need to rethink its
present carrot-and-stick strategy to win the hearts and minds of its ASEAN neighbors.
The article contributes to the literature by shedding light on the nuanced dynamics of China–
ASEAN relations through the alternative lens of news sentiment analysis. Although there has
been vibrant scholarly interest on foreign public sentiment toward China in the last decade, most
efforts rely on conventional public opinion as the primary source of data (Kang, 2010; Oh,
2021; Xie and Jin, 2022). However, studies on sentiment analysis have shown that sentiment-based
methods and language model approaches outperform traditional methods (Cheng et al., 2016; Xu
et al., 2012). Sentiment analysis can provide a more accurate and nuanced understanding of public
opinion of China, especially amidst Beijing’s aggressive effort to improve foreign public image.
Unlike public opinion data, which have longer intervals between data collection points, news articles are published regularly, allowing for the continuous monitoring of sentiment shifts. News sentiment analysis can also aggregate opinions from a wide range of sources, including reputable news
outlets and social media, allowing for a comprehensive view of public discourse. Moreover, journalists often provide analysis, expert opinions, and historical context alongside news stories, enriching the understanding of public sentiment on a particular issue. These are all timely and important in
light of China’s current dual strategy of coercion and inducement with individual ASEAN states,
which some believe have effectively undermined the ASEAN consensus principle (Le Thu, 2019).
The article proceeds as follows. We begin with a brief examination of the trade war between the
United States and China and its implications for diplomatic relations in the region amidst China’s

The US–China trade war
After China was inducted into the World Trade Organization in 2001, its foreign trade started to
grow exponentially (SCMP reporters, 2020). Headway to becoming an important global economic
powerhouse, China’s trade with the United States reached almost US$600 billion in 2019. While
China was enjoying economic growth, however, the United States had been hitting huge trade deficits with China which US politicians had attributed to China’s alleged taking advantage of the free
trade network and not opening its market wide enough for other economies (Moosa et al., 2020).
China’s putative unfair trade practices also provided a provocative political talking point for
Trump’s 2016 election campaign (BBC, 2016). When Trump became president, US foreign
policy against China became markedly aggressive. China was accused of, among other things,
undervaluing the yuan, distorting market subsidies, and stealing intellectual property (Moosa
et al., 2020; Selmi et al., 2020).
Since March 2018, the United States and China have been engaged in a series of tit-for-tat policies, levying tariffs and counter-tariffs against each other, and sending the rest of the world to
contend not just with the disruption in supply chains (Goulard, 2020) but also with higher consumer
prices (Dickler, 2019), lower corporate proﬁts (Romans, 2019), unstable markets, and slower economic growth. The United States insisted on yuan revaluation and imposing heavy tariffs even
though there were doubts these would correct US trade deﬁcits. Tariffs, which are meant to
achieve better terms of trade, for instance, generally leave no one better off when every
economy imposes it in retaliation (Ossa, 2014). Even a temporary tariff is known to have a permanent contractionary effect on the economy (Sen and Turnovsky, 1989). Moreover, it has long been
known that protectionist policies are associated not just with the decline in employment but also
with a decline in market strength (Canto et al., 1986).
Why then would economies impose tariffs and engage in a trade war? According to Liu and Woo
(2018), in the case of the United States three major concerns were immediate motivating factors: (a)
the concern that China’s chronically large trade surplus was depressing local job creation in the
United States; (b) the concern that China was using illegal and unfair methods to acquire US technology at a discount; and (c) the concern that China sought to weaken US national security and its
international standing. All three were echoed by politicians even before Trump’s election. But it
was during Trump’s presidency that the trade war escalated and the line between strategic competition and economic competition became blurred.
Economists estimate that the cost of the trade war was larger than the optimistic estimates of gain
that Trump and his administration intended to achieve (Amiti et al., 2019). Li et al.’s (2018) early
simulation shortly after the start of the trade war suggests China losing more in the fray. However,

2021). One dominant discourse in Chinese media even painted the trade war as part of the
United States’ attempt at curbing China’s rise as a global economic power (BBC, 2020).

Trade war and the ASEAN
Some analysts believe the trade war between the United States and China actually beneﬁts the
ASEAN region where ﬂedgling economies have been attracting contract manufacturing in
the last few years (Lauria, 2019). Southeast Asia is also becoming an investment hub, attracting
companies looking for lower wage alternatives, a business-friendly regulatory environment, and
a strong macroeconomic performance (Roughneed, 2019). Despite the portending economic
slump, some believe the US–China trade war could create spillovers and spell relative gains for
economies with strong trade and foreign direct investment links (Ajami, 2020). The sizeable reduction in trade between the United States and China in 2019 could in the long run lead to signiﬁcant
trade diversion, more imports from other regions, and a reorganization of value chains in most of
Asia (Bekkers and Schroeter, 2020). However, some recommend taking caution and remind that
capital ﬂight in the Chinese manufacturing sector had been taking place long before the trade
war due to China’s increasing labor cost and diminishing labor force.
Unfortunately, Southeast Asia might not be ready to absorb the diversion in the supply chain due
to poor infrastructure and logistic networks (Lei, 2019). The US–China trade war might also worsen
long-term growth uncertainties for small economies that have come to rely on either China or the
United States as their top trading partner. Pangestu (2019) cites Indonesia as an example of an
economy at the losing end but insists the prognosis can well apply to most in the ASEAN due
to the region’s still limited integration into the global value chain. Even in more structured economies such as Malaysia, policymakers are warned not to bank on trade diversion and instead to
stick to conventional policies such as intensifying investment promotions and upgrading the manufacturing sector as a vector of growth (Cheng et al., 2019). Observers believe potential gains for
emerging economies depend on what comparative advantage is left on the sectors that are
subject to the shift in demand (Carvalho et al., 2019).

Theory
This article’s theoretical anchor builds on threat perception theory and contends that economic selfinterest informs people’s perception of the other. Competing economic interests among racial or
ethnic groups produce hostile or prejudicial attitudes that are heightened during periods of economic downturns (Ghosh, 2011; Harris et al., 2018; Zatlin, 2007). Assertions of identity may be
triggered by major historical or international events which tilt the balance of public opinion
toward others. At times, the nationalist public would even interpret international disputes as affecting them personally and inducing certain personalization effects (Tam and Lee, 2018).

Although people’s critical view of other ethnic or racial groups may be based on unwarranted
qualiﬁcations or stereotypes (Burns and Gimpel, 2000), or even on apprehension about one’s
own low ﬁnancial or community status (Miller, 2016), these are further magniﬁed when the
economy is in decline, suggesting that economic scarcity impels negative prejudice (Krosch
et al., 2017). More recently, it has been suggested that during economic downturns people are
not only more likely to hold negative explicit and implicit attitudes toward other groups but also
to condone stereotypes. Downturns can even push citizens to disregard inequality as permissible
(Bianchi et al., 2018). Domestic sentiment toward a foreign entity can also entail potential audience
cost for leaders dealing with a recalcitrant or overbearing state. Quek and Johnston (2018) ﬁnd, for
example, that negative public opinion by citizens of mainland China, such as those espoused by
sectors of the public who are more hawkish toward the United States, increase instead of decrease
the domestic audience cost for Chinese leaders.
In the context of the US–China trade rivalry, as China sought to strengthen its geopolitical position, some ASEAN countries viewed its actions with suspicion. The militarization of disputed territories in the South China Sea, coupled with a more assertive foreign policy, contributed to a sense
of insecurity among certain ASEAN states. The trade war reinforced citizens’ trepidation over
China’s rise as a regional hegemon, further fueling critical sentiment of China.
China has been accused of bullying neighbors and resorting to force in dealing with its territorial
claims in Southeast Asia (Yamazaki and Osawa, 2021). Although from a realist perspective such
posturing is “reasonable” for a state on its way to becoming a global power (Kristof, 1993), the
manner by which it is asserting its economic and political relevance in the region leaves
Southeast Asian states critical and apprehensive of its expansive maritime activities. For all its
worth, the ASEAN remains an association of developing economies with limited military or economic capabilities and lacks institutional mechanisms to enforce agreements involving domestic
events or security challenges.
As these apprehensions are theoretically assumed to fuel critical appreciation of mainland China
alongside Southeast Asia’s growing uncertainty amidst the trade war, our hypothesis assumes that
US–China trade war has ampliﬁed negative sentiment toward China in the Southeast Asian news.

Data and methods
We test the aforementioned assumption in an exploratory corpus consisting of 75,619 news reports
related to China, published in the English-language websites of selected news portals in Southeast
Asia between January 2016 and March 2020.
As in extant literature, we construe press coverage of an issue of political signiﬁcance as an
accessible proxy for public opinion (Hopkins et al., 2017; Mutz and Soss, 1997). Journalists, for
instance, routinely incorporate online sentiments into narratives, especially during important
media events like election debates (McGregor, 2019). Policymakers who interpret and anticipate
public reactions to policies, for their part, engage the media by feeding not just news but government’s policy rhetoric (Wood et al., 2005). In this framework, media inﬂuence on perceptions of the
political environment serves as a basis for expectations about the state of collective opinion on an
issue (Mutz, 1997). Mass media is viewed as having signiﬁcant impact on the collective perception
of a salient issue such that increased media coverage mediates the construction of citizens’ perception of the issue as a social problem (Mutz and Soss, 1997).
Text analysis and news sentiment analysis have a long-established place in political science.
Text-based empirical approaches have been applied, among other things, to study policy-based

party competition (Laver and Garry, 2000), political trust (Mutz and Reeves, 2005), public response
to negative economic information (Soroka, 2006), the level of polarization in legislative proposals

as well as the media’s tendency to highlight the bad state of the economy contingent on the political

Text-based sentiment analysis, on the other hand, has found a range of applications in recent
years and has been used, among other things, to assess information dissemination on virus outbreaks such as that of Zika (Seltzer et al., 2017) and, more recently, COVID-19 (De Melo and
Figueiredo, 2020; Panao and Rye, 2023; Xia et al., 2022), the inclusiveness of classroom pedagogy
(Cain et al., 2020), electricity and energy pricing (Sun et al., 2020), biofuel development (Zhao
et al., 2020), and the monitoring and evaluation of public projects (Purao et al., 2012). It has
also been used to assess issues salient to speciﬁc polities, such as forecasting US economic
growth (Ardia et al., 2019), the viability of driverless cars in Denmark (Kinra et al., 2020), child
protection policies in China (Lyu and Takikawa, 2022), and family leave policy in Russia
(Kingsbury, 2018).
The news articles in this study are sampled from the following Southeast Asian states, with one
media outﬁt for each: Brunei (Borneo Bulletin), Cambodia (Khmer Times), Indonesia (Antara
News), Laos (Laotian Times), Malaysia (New Straits Times), Myanmar (Myanmar Times),
Philippines (BusinessWorld), Singapore (Channel News Asia), Thailand (Bangkok Post), and
Vietnam (Voice of Vietnam). Some of these media outﬁts are not necessarily the most subscribed.
The basis for our selection is largely impelled by two constraints: 1) language of reportage, and 2)
the presence of a digital archive that can be mined, corresponding to the period between 2016 to
2020.
The Borneo Bulletin was ﬁrst published by the Brunei Press as a community paper for expatriates in 1953. It then operated as a daily newspaper after local ﬁrm QAF acquired full ownership in
September 1990. The newspaper has the distinction of being the Sultanate’s ﬁrst English-language
daily. Although the paper had an online platform as early as the mid-1990s, access to archives and
past issues is currently restricted by a subscription paywall. It should be mentioned, however, that
despite the paper’s private ownership, mass media in Brunei are strictly controlled by the royal
family. The enforcement of a strict version of Islamic Sharia law also constrains journalists, who
must exercise self-censorship, especially when covering politics, religion, and the royal family.
The Sultanate is believed to be courting Chinese foreign direct investment to revitalize its
economy which is reeling from the drastic drop in oil prices since 2010 (Lim et al., 2023)
The Khmer Times was ﬁrst launched as an online edition in February 2014 and became a daily
newspaper in 2015. The English-language newspaper is a private outﬁt owned by Malaysian entrepreneur Mohan Tirugmanasam Bandam. However, it is worth mentioning that as early as 2018,
Cambodia had already launched a crackdown on independent media, particularly those reporting
on government corruption, forcing them to close down (Quitzon and Ear, 2023). The Khmer
Times is the only remaining English-language daily and is believed to have survived because of
its strong links to the government (Thul, 2018). China’s role as the leading investor and aid provider
to Cambodia is well documented in the literature. Over the years, China has also become
Cambodia’s most important security partner, providing the government with funds, military equipment, and personnel training (Murg, 2022).
Antara News was founded in 1937 when Indonesia was still under Dutch control, but it was
made a news agency under the control of the president of Indonesia in 1960. Afterwards, Antara
News served as a national news agency communicating government policies to the public.

The Laotian Times is an online news media outlet exclusively publishing stories straight on the

media conglomerate, and is ofﬁcially registered. However, it is not state operated. It is arguably

topics that expand beyond the scope of state-run media. Currently, the Laotian Times publishes

The New Straits Times was founded in 1845 and is arguably Malaysia’s only broadsheet
English-language newspaper. Not only is it Malaysia’s oldest newspaper still in print but it is
also one of the oldest in the Southeast Asian region. The paper is owned by Media Prima
Berhad, a publicly listed Malaysian media company and the largest media and entertainment conglomerate in Malaysia. The Media Bias / Fact Check website rates the newspaper as least biased
(with minimal bias and using very few loaded words), as well as factually and reasonably balanced
in its coverage (see e.g. Media Bias / Fact Check, n.d.).
The Myanmar Times, founded in 2000, was the longest running privately owned
English-language daily in Myanmar before its hiatus in February 2021. The paper was founded
by Australian businessman Ross Dunkley and Burmese entrepreneur Sonny Swe whose father is
a high-ranking ofﬁcer of the intelligence department. For a time, the paper was perceived as part
of the government’s propaganda machine due to Swe’s family background. Over the years,
however, it has grown to become one of the leading English-language newspapers in the
country, covering a wide range of topics including news, politics, business, culture, and sports.
In 2014, Swe was arrested and sentenced to prison on charges of violating Myanmar’s immigration
and export laws, which led to changes in the ownership structure of the newspaper. The newspaper
has since faced various challenges, including censorship and restrictions on press freedom under
Myanmar’s military regime.

The Voice of Vietnam was established as Vietnam’s national radio broadcaster on September 7,
1945, shortly after the Ho Chi Minh’s Declaration of Independence which marked the birth of
the Democratic Republic of Vietnam. During the Vietnam War (1955–1975), Voice of Vietnam
played a signiﬁcant role in broadcasting news and propaganda to both domestic and international audiences. It also provided updates on military operations, promoted solidarity among the Vietnamese
people, and highlighted the government’s perspective on the conﬂict. Following reuniﬁcation, all of
the radio stations were combined into the Voice of Vietnam, which became the national radio
station in 1978. In 1998, the Voice of Vietnam Radio published its ﬁrst daily newspaper, which also
went by the same name (Voice of Vietnam). Directly controlled by the government of Vietnam, the
Voice of Vietnam has consistently served the interests of the Vietnamese government and the
Communist Party, playing a crucial role in shaping public opinion and supporting national objectives.
As the previous discussion indicates, the media outlets included in our study vary in terms of
ownership structure. Some like Antara News and Voice of Vietnam are state controlled.
Meanwhile, Borneo Bulletin, the Khmer Times, and Channel News Asia are privately owned but
operate in an institutional environment where the state strongly shapes editorial discretion. There
are also the likes of the Laotian Times, New Straits Times, and BusinessWorld, which are run by independent publishers and operate like the proverbial free press. Since the study is concerned with foreign
policy reporting, we assume these news outﬁts to be representing the media in their respective states.
Given the aforementioned variation in ownership structure, however, admittedly this assumption
might not necessarily hold. We recognize this as among the limitations of this exploratory study.

Goldsmith and Horiuchi (2012) and included a proxy for public diplomacy operationalized as

of foreign policy (Rosati, 1988; Wish, 1980). Thus, we also take into account whether Southeast

China’s foreign ministry the previous week, as well as if these statements sought to promote
China’s cultural diplomacy. We also controlled for how COVID-19 inﬂuenced overseas perceptions of China (Silver et al., 2020).

Results
We ﬁrst obtained weekly sentiment scores by computing the average sentiment per week. The rest
of the covariates have likewise been aggregated into weekly observations (see Table 2).

Model 2 includes ﬁxed effects for each ASEAN state. As in Model 1, the relationship between
news mention of the trade war and news sentiment toward China is negative. Here, an additional
mention of the trade tension in the ASEAN press decreases public regard of China by 0.48
units, all else constant. Likewise, as in the previous model, news mention of China’s maritime
expansion in contested areas in the West Philippine Sea also draws negative sentiment.
Model 3 fully speciﬁes all the variables, the ﬁxed effects for each 10 ASEAN state, and a weekly
trend. As in models 1 and 2, news mention of China’s trade war with the United States elicits negative sentiment. In this case, an additional news mention drives negative sentiment by 0.46 units.
Similarly, an additional news mention of China’s row in the West Philippine Sea also induces critical feelings of China by 0.5 units. It can be observed that in all three models the effect of the trade
war on public perception toward China is consistent. They all suggest that, in general, the trade war
elicits mainly critical narrative by the ASEAN press, in line with our hypothesis.
News of China’s territorial encroachment on disputed territories claimed by other ASEAN states
also adds to the negative perception, although this effect is observed only in Models 2 and 3. The
negative sentiment is consistent with those held by a number of archipelagic states in Southeast
Asia that have long perceived China as a security threat (Kim and Gates, 2015; Odgaard, 2003).
After Deng Xiaoping, China has pursued a more assertive stance in the South China Sea, raising
concerns among ASEAN countries and prompting them to deliberately seek the support of other
superpowers (Hong, 2013). In October 1995, former Philippine president Fidel V Ramos even
appealed to the United States to retain its military presence in Asia on the pretext that
Beijing’s then lack of capability did not mean it would not pose a military and political threat
to Southeast Asia.
The estimates also show that news on the COVID-19 pandemic stirred a more critical opinion
of China, echoing what some international media refer to as “hostility” over China’s coronavirus
response (Zheng, 2020). When COVID-19 broke out in January 2020, blame was largely pinned
on China not only because the virus was ﬁrst reported in Wuhan but also because of the Chinese
government’s lack of transparency and unwillingness to share information about the virus (Yang
and Chen, 2021). When the pandemic spread to the rest of the world, China attempted to redeem
its reputation by actively publicizing aid sent to affected countries (Wong, 2020). Unfortunately,
China’s mask diplomacy seemed to have backﬁred and aggravated adverse foreign public
opinion about the country (Verma, 2020).
Interestingly, statements from China’s Ministry of Foreign Affairs meant to promote the country’s cultural diplomacy, even those statements relevant to the ASEAN or member states, do not
seem to make a dent toward creating a favorable image. The ﬁndings lend credence to the observation that while China’s state propaganda machine works at home, it fails to convince hearts
and minds overseas, not even those of ASEAN neighbors (Yip, 2018).
What appears to work for China are high-level visits to and from China. These ofﬁcial visits
seem to create a positive impact on the ASEAN press. High-level state visits are well known in
the literature as vectors of nation branding (Goldsmith and Horiuchi, 2012; Goldstein, 2008) and
they seem to bode well for China too. It seems that China’s own Foreign Affairs Ministry is
aware of this, as right after Pew Research released its report showing global sentiment on China
at its record low (Silver et al., 2020), China’s foreign affairs announced a series of goodwill
visits to Southeast Asia as part of efforts “to deepen cooperation with ASEAN, uphold multilateralism and maintain peace and stability” (Wu, 2020). These high-level visits are usually accompanied
by trade and investment deals or generous aid packages and are resorted to by other major powers as
part of their checkbook diplomacy (Wang and Stone, 2023).

Conclusion
While apprehension about China among neighbors in the ASEAN region is nothing new, our ﬁndings show that economic uncertainties amidst the trade war had magniﬁed collective apprehension
of China, adding to the critical perception among the ASEAN online press. As with conventional
accounts, our ﬁndings also indicate that news of China’s territorial encroachment on disputed
territories claimed by other ASEAN states creates a negative opinion. Likewise, news on the
COVID-19 pandemic adds to the negative sentiment toward China.
It is not difﬁcult to see why. Despite a number of ASEAN states carving their development trajectories on the back of the Chinese economy, global perception of China remains largely mixed.
As China faced off with the United States, slapping tariffs one after the other, Southeast Asian states
on the sidelines became at risk of being casualties or collateral damage. At the domestic level, precarious security and the bad state of the economy are closely intertwined. As mass perception of
external threat increased, people were inclined to project in-group cohesion and adopt an aggressive
or critical response to the outside group seen as the source of threat (Lavine et al., 2005;
Moskalenko et al., 2006). It will be recalled that not so long ago, citizens of mainland China projected similar mass perception, when deteriorating images of the United States and Japan created a
vicious cycle of negative news coverage (Sinkkonen and Elovainio, 2020).
The geopolitical dimensions of the US–China trade rivalry also likely contributed to negative
sentiment within Southeast Asia. China’s assertiveness in territorial disputes, particularly in the
South China Sea, seemed to have heightened anxiety among Southeast Asian nations about its geopolitical intentions and the potential for a shift in the regional power balance. The perception of
economic uncertainty due to the trade war, coupled with anxiety over China’s expanding inﬂuence
in the region, only raised further alarms. Of course, it is possible that China had been misunderstood. A World Trade Organization ruling in September 2020 suggests China had the moral
high ground, ﬁnding that some US tariffs against China violated trading rules (Zumbrun and
Michaels, 2020). The US Trade Representative criticized the decision, arguing it was based on inadequate provisions. The decision did little, however, to dissipate what inevitably became a growing
distrust of China. It did not seem to help either that the Chinese leadership continues to ignore
global reproach and construes public diplomacy as a simple strategic and functional instrument
devoid of mutual commitments (Hartig, 2016).
Be that as it may, we recognize a number of limitations which we hope can be addressed in future
iterations of this study. News sentiment analysis provides a relatively straightforward process compared to gathering opinions through surveys or interviews because most news articles are publicly
available and easily accessible. However, news outlets may also prioritize certain topics or viewpoints over others, or underrepresent certain demographic groups in news coverage, leading to a
biased representation of public sentiment (Mach et al., 2021). Additionally, in the context of illiberal regimes that has come to characterize Southeast Asia, the press operates under strict control and
censorship by the ruling authority, becoming the mouthpiece of government instead of mirroring
public opinion (Siebert et al., 1963). This might be why the state-controlled press in countries
such as Vietnam counterintuitively indicate positive news sentiment despite the surveys showing
otherwise. Hanoi is known to ﬁlter news that might pique Beijing—an ideological comrade and
a key trading partner—be it news on protests against China’s draconian COVID-19 measures
(Vietnamese Magazine, 2022) or anti-China comments by Vietnamese netizens (Nguyen and
Luong, 2020). News outlets may also prioritize sensational or attention-grabbing headlines,
which may not accurately reﬂect the true sentiment of the public, and lead to an overemphasis

of extreme viewpoints and distortions. In an earlier analysis, Mutz and Soss (1997) found that the
salience of news sentiment is indirect, i.e. limited in terms of shaping public opinion though instrumental in inﬂuencing the perceptual environment in which policy debates transpire. Overall, while
news sentiment analysis can provide valuable insights into public opinion, it should be used with a
fair amount of caution and in conjunction with other methods to ensure a more comprehensive
understanding of public sentiment.
In conclusion, our analysis of news sentiment shows that threat perception encompassing economic and geopolitical dimensions played a pivotal role in shaping negative global opinions about
China during its trade war with the United States. The interconnectedness of the global economy
made it clear to small economies in the ASEAN that the repercussions of the trade war extended
beyond the primary actors, generating widespread concern about economic stability, and consequently negative sentiment toward Asia’s rising hegemon.
