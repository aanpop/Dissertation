ABSTRACT

Since 2013, China has undertaken extensive reclamation and construction on
several reefs in the Spratly Island chain in the South China Sea. China has since
been adding new construction and fortifications to the land features that had
undergone reclamation between late 2013 and 2015, but none of the new projects
rivalled the large-scale reclamation from 2014–15, and nor has China reclaimed any
new land features. Land reclamation has theoretical and empirical implications. The
literature regarding maritime disputes in the South China Sea is rich and plenty.
Many studies, however, focus on China’s bilateral assertive or coercive behaviour as
well as China’s general strategy in the South China Sea, not land reclamation per se.
What explains the timing of China’s large-scale land reclamation in the South China
Sea? Why did China decide to initiate large-scale land reclamation in the first place?
This paper, therefore, conducts a comprehensive case study analysing China’s land
reclamation decisions. I argue that China’s land reclamation is a result of capability,
rationale, and opportunities which include a calculation of U.S. resolve. This study
has implications for understanding Chinese foreign policy decision-making and
contributes to the credibility debate in the coercion literature.
KEYWORDS China; maritime; security; credibility; resolve

Introduction
Since 2013, China has undertaken extensive reclamation and construction on
several reefs in the Spratly Island chain in the South China Sea, which has resulted
in over 2,000 acres (809 hectares) of artificial landmasses on Chinese-occupied
reefs that are disputed between several countries, including Vietnam, the
Philippines, and Malaysia.1 China announced on 16 June 2015 that its reclama­
tion work would be completed ‘in the upcoming days’, and that when reclama­
tion was finished, it would build facilities on the newly created artificial Islands.2

China had long engaged in land reclamation in the South China Sea,
although not on a large scale. In 1991, China allowed its Navy to construct
airports on Woody Islands in the Paracel Islands.4 China seized and built
infrastructure on Mischief Reef in 1995 and refurbished the infrastructure in
1998. Chinese land reclamation activities then stopped altogether and did not
resume until the end of 2013, on an unprecedented scale. The fact that China
built infrastructure on land features in the 1990s and came to a halt in the 2000s
is in line with the general trend of Chinese reactive and immediate coercion.5
Yet interestingly, China’s land reclamation activity started in 2013, took off in
2014, and then gradually paused in 2015. IHS Jane’s monitored the movements
of the Chinese dredger Tian Jing Hao and found it located on-station at Johnson
South Reef on 17 December 2013 and land reclamation operations began at
Johnson South Reef on 26 February 2014.6 This Chinese dredger subsequently
moved to the Hughes Reef, Gaven Reefs, Fiery Cross Reefs, and other reefs in
2014 and 2015.7 Based on satellite images, China reclaimed about 13 square
kilometres of land on its seven land features in the Spratly Islands between
2014 and 2015.8 The size of Chinese land reclamation in 2014 and 2015 is so
large that it dwarfs the land reclamation of all other claimants combined.

The interesting questions about the empirical details and theoretical
implications of China’s large-scale land reclamation are understudied. That
is, what explains the timing of China’s large-scale land reclamation in the
South China Sea? Why did China decide to initiate large-scale land reclama­
tion in the first place? China took control of one land feature in the mid-1990s,
then stopped, and did not start large-scale Island reclamation until 2014. Why
did China start in late 2013, turn to large-scale reclamation in 2014, and stop
around 2015? Why did China never reclaim the land feature that is under its
de-facto control, the Scarborough Shoal? Equally important, what is the
rationale behind China’s land reclamation? Is it expansionist? There is, there­
fore, room for theorizing and analysing China’s land-reclamation activities in
depth, especially in relation to serval alternative explanations, such as
a purely capability-based argument and a bureaucratic or domestic lobbyistsbased argument. The former argues that China’s actions are a result of
capability growth, whereas the latter attributes China’s behaviour to influ­
ence from domestic bureaucracies and lobbies.20 The purpose of this paper is
not to pick a winner among the factors but to identify a comprehensive list of
key explanatory factors critical for explaining the rationale and timing of
China’s land reclamation by tracking the decision-making process in detail.
Using primary Chinese-language sources and interviews with former civi­
lian and military officials, I argue that China’s decision to engage in large-scale
land reclamation in the South China Sea in 2014 resulted from a combination
of necessary conditions and opportunities. First, the rationale for land recla­
mation manifested itself during the CCP’s 16th Party Congress in June 2012 to
further develop the oceans (Haiyang kaifa) and the subsequent emphasis on
strengthening logistical capacities of offshore Islands (haidao kaifa) in March
2013. Second, China’s capability for land reclamation was not mature until
around 2012. Third, it was only beginning in 2012 that China reduced its land
reclamation projects in the coastal regions of mainland China, which freed up
resources, including dredgers necessary for large-scale land reclamation in
the South China Sea. Fourth, China chose 2014 to ramp up its land reclama­
tion efforts because it inferred the level of U.S. credibility and commitment
from its lukewarm response regarding Syria and Ukraine, therefore interpret­
ing the United States as lacking resolve in militarily challenging China directly
if China increased its land reclamation efforts in the South China Sea. China,
by and large, paused its large-scale land reclamation by 2015 because the
majority of the construction was finished. However, China did not proceed to
reclaim the Scarborough Shoal because of the clear signals sent by the
Obama administration. In short, while land reclamation, from the CCP’s
perspective, is a logical extension of its strategy of maritime development,

This paper adds to the theoretical debate on signalling and credibility
and suggests that past actions do matter to a state’s adversary when the
adversary calculates the resolve and commitment of the state in defend­
ing a particular issue. It highlights the interconnectedness of credibility,
that is, lack of action in one event may be perceived as lack of commit­
ment in general, thereby reducing the state’s credibility in the eyes of its
adversary. Relatedly, states may take assertive or even coercive actions
precisely for reasons of the interconnectedness of credibility, the belief
that actions in one event may have a deterrent effect on future events. In
other words, capability is relevant, but is not the only factor for explain­
ing states’ behaviour.
Moreover, the paper contributes to the literature on the South China Sea
and Chinese foreign policy, adding more nuance to our understanding of the
decision-making process of China and refuting the argument that it is bureau­
cratic politics that led to China’s land reclamation. A detailed case study on
land reclamation may have implications for our analysis of China’s foreign
policy decision-making, such as under what conditions does China consider
certain policies and what is the underlying rationale behind their execution.
Finally, the paper has policy implications for how to maintain peace and
stability in the Asia-Pacific region as well as to manage China’s rise. It sheds
light on the decision-making rationale of China and under what conditions
China is more likely to take assertive actions. Empirically, China’s land recla­
mation in the South China Sea is of great concern to foreign policy decision­
makers and could be potentially destabilizing. Despite the conventional
assumption that states will become more assertive as they become more
powerful, it is unclear what specific capacities, conditions, and opportunities
might be relevant for assertive behaviour. It is, therefore, critical to under­
stand when and why China will initiate or stop land reclamation.
Regarding methods and sources, this is a qualitative case study using
process tracing to assess China’s decision-making process. I use both primary
and secondary sources to triangulate to ensure the trustworthiness of the
evidence presented and the relative objectivity of the research, therefore
contributing to empirical studies of maritime disputes as well as our under­
standing of the details of China’s foreign policy decision-making. Some of the
primary sources are official government documents and speeches, including
the annual book (zhongguo waijiao) from the Chinese Ministry of Foreign
Affairs, hereafter the MFA. I also use official statements, press briefings from
the MFA, and the People’s Daily. I use official documents from foreign coun­
tries to cross-check against Chinese documents. Moreover, I use the following
semi-official reports from government think tanks: the China Ocean

Nevertheless, while capability is a necessary condition for land reclamation, it
does not represent the full picture. We need to examine the rationale (or lack
thereof) of land reclamation.

Rationale
Regarding rationale, one would examine Chinese government documents
and scientific research conducted by Chinese scientists prior to the initiation
of land reclamation to assess what are the major drivers. The following
passages first discuss the decision-making timeline and then examine the
rationale in detail.

Decision-making timeline
With the technological conditions ripe, the Chinese government began to
place the development of maritime resources on its top agenda. The deci­
sion-making regarding land reclamation involves two steps: 1) considering
land reclamation as an option and 2) devising a specific plan of land
reclamation.
First, China started to consider land reclamation as an option in 2012. In
April 2012, then Chinese Premier Wen Jiabao noted the need to devise

Official documents from local Hainan Province as well as research done by
Chinese scientists were useful pieces of evidence. According to an aforemen­
tioned document from the Hainan Province, the rationale was three-fold:
military, administrative, and economic, with the aim being to provide military

are not post-hoc justification or rationalisation. In short, late 2013 and espe­
cially early 2014 provided China with a geopolitical loophole – the perceived
military pressure from the United States was lower. The following passages
utilise interview data to further substantiate this argument.
Chinese scholars and former diplomats noted that even though China had
started land reclamation in late 2013, its large-scale reclamation began in
early to mid-2014, because of President Obama’s reaction with regard to the
Ukrainian crisis in 2014.91 That is, China read the lack of U.S. counter-reactions
during the Ukrainian crisis as a sign that Obama was weak, and the logic was
such that if the United States did not use force despite the Ukrainian issue, it
certainly would not take drastic measures against China over land
reclamation.92 China therefore jumped through the loophole (zuan kongzi),
engaging in land reclamation when Obama was still in his tenure and when
the Ukrainian crisis erupted.93 The Ukrainian crisis, therefore, provided
a favourable international environment for China.94 Another government
policy analyst agreed that the internal discussion back in 2014 was that the
Middle East was in chaos, as was Ukraine, and that Obama was mild (wenhe),
indicating that Chinese policy analysts believed a military backlash from the
United States over land reclamation would be quite unlikely.95 Several senior
China government policy analysts and former diplomats summarised China’s
rationale: convinced that the United States would not go to war with China
over issues in the South China Sea, the particularly low military involvement
from the United States seemed to have given China the green light for land
reclamation.96
One might wonder why Ukraine and Syria are relevant for the South China
Sea. In the 2012 Scarborough incident, Chinese analysts believe that if China
used grey-zone coercion in the South China Sea, then the United States
would not get militarily involved.97 U.S. response in the Scarborough incident
confirmed China’s perception. Land reclamation, however, is different from
Chinese Coast Guard ships ramming foreign vessels in the South China Sea
because it involves making significant changes to disputed pieces of territory.
In this sense, the Ukraine crisis is a relevant and logical event for Chinese
analysts to observe because Russia engaged in actions to make changes to

Alternative explanations
Some might argue that China’s land reclamation started because of Xi Jinping,
thereby subscribing to an individual leadership-based explanation.110
However, China had been planning to reclaim land in 2012, which was during
Hu’s reign.111 It was a continuous policy and not something that took place
only after Xi came to power.112 Another former diplomat also stressed that land
reclamation was not a result of individual leaders.113 As one government policy
analyst specialising in South China Sea issues concluded, the leadership is not
the main factor; it is the cost analysis that explains Chinese behaviour.114
Related is the argument that hawkish leaders will be more prone to use
assertive actions, despite adverse international and domestic environments.
Among Jiang Zemin, Hu Jintao, and Xi Jinping, Hu is said to have been the
weakest leader and Xi the most assertive.115 Yet China again began taking
assertive measures in 2007, during Hu’s term.116 In an internal speech during
the Central Foreign Affairs Conference in August 2006, Hu stated that ‘China
needed to be more proactive in foreign affairs,’ which undermines the notion
that it was Xi that emphasised proaction.117 One of Xi’s former political secretaries
revealed that Xi’s viewpoints were highly in line with the centre.118 Interviews
with Chinese government analysts also confirm that individual leadership does
not dictate coercion decisions.119 One government policy analyst indicated that
all decisions about coercion were decided at the PBSC and individual leadership
was not crucial.120 In short, individual Chinese leaders are not indispensable in
explaining when China takes actions in the South China Sea.

Conclusion
Having been planned for a long time, Chinese land reclamation is a deliberate and
proactive action. China’s land reclamation is a complex event resulting from
a combination of necessary conditions, rational motivations, and opportunities.
China decides to place artificial structures on land features it occupies in the South
China Sea to increase logistical capacities that serve military, economic, and
administrative purposes. China, however, was unable to start land reclamation
any time it wanted to. Rather, the technical conditions for land reclamation were
not ready till around 2012. It was only after 2012 that China reduced its near-shore
land reclamation projects in the coastal regions of mainland China, which freed
up resources, including dredgers necessary for large-scale land reclamation in the
South China Sea. It was also in 2012 that scientific research on maritime land
reclamation matured. Finally, despite beginning the land reclamation project in
September 2013, China chose 2014 to step up its land reclamation efforts because
it inferred the level of U.S. credibility and commitment from its lukewarm
response regarding Syria and Ukraine, therefore interpreting the United States
as lacking resolve to militarily challenge China directly if China increased its land
reclamation efforts in the South China Sea. China, by and large, paused its largescale land reclamation by 2015 because the majority of the construction was
finished. However, China did not proceed to reclaim the Scarborough Shoal
because of the clear signals sent by the Obama administration. In short, while
land reclamation, from the CCP’s perspective, is a logical extension of its strategy
of maritime development, there were necessary conditions and opportunities
that led to China’s large-scale land reclamation.
Theoretically, there are many capability-based theories on rising power
behaviour. One strand of the literature on rising power behaviour is offensive
realism, which predicts that rising powers bide their time and become
increasingly aggressive in pursuing regional hegemony as they become
more capable.132 The logical extension, applied to land reclamation, is that
China will pursue land reclamation when its capability is ready. This paper
demonstrates that, however, what matters for explanatory purposes is not
overall Chinese power such as economic indicators, but rather the specific
technology and the availability of it that made land reclamation possible.
Related to the capability argument is the debate within the literature on
credibility regarding the sources of credibility. Daryl Press, on one hand,
argues that states calculate the credibility of their adversaries based on their
current capabilities instead of inferring credibility from past actions.133 On
the other hand, the literature on reputation and signalling views past
actions, especially acquiring a reputation of being credible, as important.

This line of thinking that focuses on past behaviour comes from Thomas
Schelling and Robert Jervis. Schelling views credibility as a bank, treating
precedents – past actions – as important to maintain one’s credibility.134
Therefore, Schelling calls for the importance of communicating ‘evidence’ of
commitment, which requires more than the communication of words.135 He
further argues that actions are more credible than verbal messages: actions
prove something and incur some cost or risk.136 In short, a reputation for
resolve goes beyond a particular event per se. It has implications for other
issues and the state’s reputation vis-à-vis other states. Therefore, states
might be observing the other state’s behaviour elsewhere to infer that
state’s resolve. This paper affirms Schelling’s argument and counters
Press’s argument that states use ‘current capability’ to assess adversaries’
resolve and credibility. China, on the contrary, had been watching
U.S. statements and the lukewarm reactions to the Ukraine Crisis before
going ahead with large-scale land reclamation in May 2014. In particular,
China’s attention to Ukraine and Syria demonstrates the ‘interconnected­
ness’ of credibility. Empirically, this paper adds to the literature on Chinese
foreign policy and maritime disputes, especially providing more nuance to
China’s decision-making process. States such as China, Russia, and North
Korea, may be highly sensitive to past U.S. actions and statements. One
policy implication for the United States is that it should be cautious about
its past statements and actions not just in the Asia-Pacific region but
around the world, making sure it is consistent and resolved. Otherwise,
U.S. adversaries might interpret the lack of U.S. actions or inconsistent
statements as lacking credibility of resolve.
Finally, the rationale behind China’s land reclamation suggests a goldilocks
kind of choice that China has been making. On one hand, China intends land
reclamation to enhance its military and economic positions in the South
China Sea. Compared to grabbing new pieces of territory in the South
China Sea, land reclamation is assertive but not overly assertive. As such, it
helps China defend its territorial interests without incurring dramatic geopo­
litical costs. On the other hand, China has domestic economic developmental
interests and must balance coastal development and land reclamation in the
South China Sea. This indicates that as a rising power keen on growing its
economy, China still faces finite resources and must balance domestic and
external goals.137 As the grand strategy literature suggests, states face finite
