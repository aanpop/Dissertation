How does geopolitical uncertainty affect Chinese overseas investment in
the energy sector? Evidence from the South China Sea Dispute

Based on Chinese outward foreign direct investment data, this study examines the impact of the South China Sea
Dispute on China’s overseas investments in oil & gas-related sectors. The South China Sea Dispute alarms a
potential threat to China’s energy supply. By applying a difference-in-difference estimation, we find that the
South China Sea Dispute brings about an increase in the frequency of China’s oil & gas-related investments to
low-risk countries. The findings are robust under several specification tests. It indicates that under geopolitical
uncertainty environment, energy-importers will reallocate energy investment in order to avoid the potential
disruption of energy supply.

1. Introduction
Uncertainty is one of the major factors that influence the investment
decision of business operators. Under turbulent environment of uncer­
tainty, firms may alter their investment and financing decisions to avoid
potential losses. The outbreak of extreme events such as epidemics and
geopolitical events increases uncertainty of the business environment,
which triggers change in investment patterns in affected areas. In the
past century, disputes of the South China Sea have risen mistrust and
aggravating tension in the region. In order to avoid the unforeseen
negative effect for better decision making, it is important to identify the
effect of geopolitical uncertainty on the overseas investment of China’s
energy firms.
The South China Sea is a semi-enclosed sea located in the Indian
Pacific region, stretching south to the Strait of Malacca in Singapore and
Malaysia, and north to the Taiwan Strait. The Strait of Malacca, which
connects the Indian Ocean with the Pacific Ocean through the South
China Sea, serves as the shortest route between the Persian Gulf and
Asian markets. As a major route for international trade, the South China
Sea carries vital importance to China. It is perceived as the throat of
global sea routes (Holmes, 2014; Kaplan, 2011). Moreover, the Strait of
Malacca is described as the Achilles’ heel for China’s energy security
(Kelanic, 2016; Wu, 2008; Yang et al., 2015). According to the U.S.
Energy Information Administration, more than 30% of the global crude

oil trade transit through the South China Sea in 2016, and more than
90% of China’s crude oil volumes flow through the South China Sea in
2016 (EIA, 2018).
Major geopolitical events in the South China Sea will possibly lead to
permanent change in the patterns of China’s maritime trade and affect
China’s oil & gas-related OFDI. With the increase in energy demand,
China faces domestic supply constraint that could only be addressed via
oil imports. The peaceful environment in the South China Sea is neces­
sary for China’s oil shipping. Contrarily, any potential risks in the South
China Sea would influence China’s oil imports and threaten national
energy security. Aggravating tension in this area would impede or even
halt regional commerce, leading to a disruption of economic develop­
ment in East Asia countries, especially for China (Creehan, 2012;
Gueldry and Liang, 2016; Lee, 2019; Li et al., 2014; Zhong and White,
2017). This study analyses the South China Sea Dispute and investigates
whether China considers political risk in its overseas energy investment.
The motivation of this study has two aspects. Firstly, the South China
Sea Dispute provides an exogenous setting for empirical analysis. Since
economic policies relatively change with the macro environment as
uncertainty increases, the effect of uncertainty is mixed. Conversely, the
South China Sea Dispute did not trigger war nor has real effect on the
economy of the claimant states. Thus, this study uses the escalation
between claimant states in 2010 as an exogenous shock to examine the
impact of geopolitical uncertainty. The identification strategy will help

events on energy security. Access to reliable and sufficient energy re­

Therefore, energy-importing economies need to combat the negative
impact of extreme events regarding energy security.
This study is associated with two strands of literature. One studies
how firms make investment decisions under uncertain environment
(Bernanke, 1983; Caballero and Pindyck, 1992; Dixit et al., 1994). When
there is an increase in political uncertainty, firms will adopt the waitand-see strategy and delay making sunk investments owing to the irre­
versible nature of the investment. The negative correlations between
uncertainty and investment have been found during periods of external
shocks and political turnovers (An et al., 2016; Chen et al., 2020b; Gu
et al., 2021; Haroon and Rizvi, 2020; He et al., 2020b, 2020a; Jens,
2017; Julio and Yook, 2012; Mishra et al., 2020; Njindan Iyke, 2020; Sha
and Sharma, 2020; Vidya and Prabheesh, 2020). Other research has also
found reduced economic activities and depressed stock prices caused by
political uncertainty (Al Mamun et al., 2020; Balcilar et al., 2018; Cal­
dara and Iacoviello, 2017; Chen et al., 2016; Dissanayake et al., 2018;
Fania et al., 2020; Gu et al., 2020; Samuel et al., 2020; Shen et al., 2020;
Wang et al., 2019). Extant studies mainly use domestic political turnover
or deterioration in bilateral relations to describe geopolitical uncer­
tainty, while the impact of regional geopolitical events and how un­
certainty affects investment behaviour remain under discussion. This
study is to analyse whether firms reallocate their investment when
facing geopolitical threats.
On the other hand, this study is associated with the issue of energy
security and national policy (Apergis and Apergis, 2020; Cao and Bluth,
2013; Devpura and Narayan, 2020; Fu and Shen, 2020; Ge and Fan,
2013; Gil-Alana and Monge, 2020; Liu et al., 2020b; Narayan, 2020; Qin
et al., 2020; Vivoda, 2009; Vivoda and Manicom, 2011; Wu, 2014).
Countries that rely heavily on foreign energy supply would face rela­
tively high risk of supply disruption once extreme events happen at the
international energy market. Taking China for instance, the excess
dependence on particular oil-producing countries and specific trans­
portation routes have put China at a vulnerable stage (Li et al., 2020;
Odgaard and Delman, 2014; Zhang, 2011). To maintain a stable supply,
importers tend to keep a good diplomatic relation with oil-producing
countries. Lee (2019) points out the “oil for ally” paradigm, in which
importers often take the energy endowment of the partner country into
consideration and possibly give preferential foreign policies to its
resource-exporting partners. Researchers have also emphasized the
close relationship of China’s foreign policy to its energy cooperation
partner (Gueldry and Liang, 2016; Lee, 2019; Li et al., 2014). As per
above facts, the outbreak of potential geopolitical event will alarm great
threat to China’s energy security. However, the relationship between
geopolitical events and energy investment has been rarely studied
empirically. We fill this gap by linking geopolitical uncertainty to energy
investment with China’s outward FDI data. We explore whether China
adopts the diversification strategy and overseas energy investment to
address the concern of energy resources.
This study uses the South China Sea Dispute in 2010 as a quasiexperiment to examine the impact of geopolitical uncertainty on
China’s OFDI in the energy sector. For stable energy importing, China
will diverge investment into less risky countries. The finding shows that
the South China Sea Dispute leads to 0.72 increase in the frequency of
China’s oil & gas-related investments to low-risk countries. Robustness
checks including DDD and subsample analysis are conducted to verify
the baseline results. Firstly, a DDD estimation is applied to eliminate
unobservable factors regarding investment regulations. We add a
dimension on the sector level and find that the deregulation of foreign
investment will not affect the robustness of the results. Secondly, we
conduct a subsample analysis for countries that have both trade and
investment connections with China. Since some countries have no in­
vestment connection with China throughout the period, we exclude

these countries to ensure that the establishment of the investment
project is related to energy trade. Thirdly, we conduct a subsample
analysis for countries within close geographical distance. Since coun­
tries in Africa and America are geographically far away from China, we
exclude those countries to rule out the impact of concurrent events in
remote areas. Moreover, we conduct a subsample analysis for invest­
ment projects from central State-Owned-Enterprises. Since investments
proposed by the central government are aimed at implementing national
policies, we only keep these investments to capture the purpose of
China’s OFDI. Finally, we re-estimate the results using alternative
models such as the negative binomial model and the Poisson model.
Results from the above robustness checks all support the main findings
of this study.
This paper complements extant literature in two aspects. Firstly, we
propose an ideal exogenous shock to study the impact of geopolitical
events on investment. Existing studies have discussed the positive cor­
relation between the geopolitical events and investment (Caldara and
Iacoviello, 2017; Chen et al., 2016; Dissanayake et al., 2018). Never­
theless, they fail to isolate the pure geopolitical event effect to establish
a credible causal result. We complement the extant literature on
geopolitical events with the South China Sea Dispute. As an exogenous
shock, it did not trigger war nor have real effect on the economy of the
claimant states. Thus, we are able to analyse pure risk while excluding
changes in the business environment in the host country. Secondly, we
extend the understanding of geopolitical threats and national energy
security. As a major energy-importer in the world, China faces severe
challenges of supply disruption once the maritime routes are interrupted
(Li et al., 2020; Odgaard and Delman, 2014; Zhang, 2011). This study
explores the impact of geopolitical event on the hedging behaviour of
Chinese overseas investments. The finding shows that the South China
Sea Dispute diversifies China’s oil & gas-related investment towards
low-risk countries.
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: Section 2 in­
troduces the empirical strategy, Section 3 and Section 4 report estima­
tion results and Section 5 concludes.
2. Empirical strategy
2.1. Sample design
This study intends to examine the impact of geopolitical uncertainty
on the location choice of China’s OFDI in the energy sector. Data on
China’s OFDI are collected from China’s Ministry of Commerce. The
micro database from the Ministry of Commerce, which is widely
acknowledged in previous research regarding China, provides records
on the approval of each outbound investment up to 2015 (Luo et al.,
2017; Sun et al., 2020). The database includes the approval date, the
name of the investor, the host country and the business description of
each investment. However, it does not record the amount of each in­
vestment. This study focuses on the projects that have mentioned ac­
tivities such as oil & gas exploration and production in their business
descriptions. Besides, we exclude countries involved with the South
China Sea Dispute from the sample. The full sample consists of 78
countries that have crude oil trade relation with China from 2007 to
2015. Data on economic indicators of each country are gathered from
the World Bank and International Monetary Fund. Geographical infor­
mation of each country is collected from CEPII. All the continuous
variables are winsorized at the 1st and 99th percentiles.
2.2. Model setting
This study takes the South China Sea Dispute as an exogenous shock
in a difference-in-difference (DID) framework. The identification strat­
egy is similar to Chen et al. (2016) which uses the China-Japan Island
Dispute as an exogenous shock. Generally, the RCT method is also
suitable for evaluating the impact of an intervention or policy shocks.

However, in this study, the DID method is superior to the RCT method in
several aspects. Firstly, the DID method is more relevant to the real
world. It provides a more feasible and practical way in our setting.
Secondly, the DID method could explain external validity through sta­
tistical designs. The parallel trend test is conducted before the DID test in
order to eliminate selection bias.
This study uses the policy shift of the U.S. in the South China Sea in
2010 to explore the impact of geopolitical uncertainty. At the ASEAN
Regional Forum in 2010, the United States expressed concern about
China’s claims and policies. The U.S. Secretary Hillary Clinton claimed
“legitimate claims to maritime space in the South China Sea is derived
solely from legitimate claims to land features” (DOS, 2010). The
declaration in 2010 marks the turning point of U.S. policy towards the
South China Sea, after which the South China Sea Dispute has emerged
into a major issue worldwide (Beckman, 2010; Yang et al., 2021). The
involvement of the U.S. is regarded as the “pivot to Asia” policy of the
Obama administration and has upped the stakes for China (Ba, 2011;
Buszynski, 2012; Fravel, 2016; Ling, 2013). The revision in the position
of the United States has possibly fuelled potential conflicts for claimant
states in the region. For instance, more frequent ship collisions took
place in 2010, and the South China Sea Arbitration was applied after­
wards. The trend is depicted in Fig. 1 in the Appendix. From 2004 to
2009, the online keyword searching of “South China Sea” worldwide in
the Google Trend Index had remained flat, indicating that the conflicts
have maintained at a low and manageable level. Since 2010, the line has
displayed a sharp rise with substantial variation and extreme volatility.
Frequent conflicts between the claimant states have led to regional un­
certainty and geopolitical unsafety in the South China Sea. In the sam­
ple, the host countries are divided into the treatment group and the
control group based on whether the South China Sea Dispute threatens
the bilateral crude oil shipping route. Since crude oil trade between
neighbouring land countries is generally transported via railway or
pipeline, these countries are regarded as low-risk oil-exporting econo­
mies (Lai, 2007; Wang et al., 2020; Zhu et al., 2015). A country is
assigned to the control group if the shipping route flow through the
South China Sea. An alternative control group is also applied as a
robustness check. The basic control group includes countries other than
neighbours in the Central Asia, while the alternative control group in­
cludes countries other than neighbours in the Central and South Asia.

by year fixed effect.
In the model, δi and δt represents country fixed effect and year fixed
effect respectively. The panel data set may suffer from serial correlation
problem. To solve this issue, standard errors clustered at the country
level are used.

5. Conclusion
Stable energy supply is one of the most indispensable drivers of
China’s economic growth. However, the frequent occurrence of extreme
events has emerged as a new source of instability and posed funda­
mental challenges to China’s energy security. In some severe cases,
extreme events could threaten not only China’s energy trade but may
trigger a global energy crisis.
Taking the South China Sea Dispute as an exogenous shock, this
study examines the impact of the geopolitical event on China’s overseas
energy investment patterns in a DID framework. The finding shows that
the South China Sea Dispute brings about an increase in China’s oil &
gas-related investment intent to low-risk countries. The results are
robust to specification checks. Increasing tension caused by the South
China Sea Dispute significantly alters the decision to whom China want
to work with in energy cooperation. Consequently, China will diverge its
investment intent to countries with lower risk route exposure.
This study underscores that in the face of geopolitical uncertainty,
energy-importing countries tend to reallocate investment to mitigate the
potential risk of energy supply disruption. The findings have illumi­
nating policy implications: Firstly, to cope with novel global and
geopolitical challenges in the energy sector, diversification strategy will
help the government to promote societal stability and security benefits.
Through diversification of supply sources, energy portfolio and trans­
port route, governments can manage energy insecurity. Secondly, in
regional contexts, cooperation and integration are vital to enhancing
energy security. Governments should promote further cooperation,
including energy infrastructure development and energy market
convergence among neighbouring economies to achieve regional con­
nectivity. Possible synergies of energy efficiency and energy potential
would rise, followed by the thicker interlinkage among countries in the
region, which could reduce potential conflicts. Thirdly, governments
should commit to maintaining stable diplomatic and commercial re­
lations with primary energy exporting countries in order to ensure the
reliability of energy supply. Bilateral collaborations such as advanced
research projects and business opportunity forums could be conducive to
the reliable flow of energy as well as the long-term economic well-being
of both countries.

4.4. Keeping investments from central SOEs
In the sample, the motivation behind China’s oil & gas-related OFDI
may not be promoting bilateral energy trade. In other words, China’s
OFDI may be aimed at business motives, such as profit from construction
and building infrastructure. Distinguishing firm ownership and invest­
ment motivation will help eliminate irrelevant OFDI. Generally, local
SOEs are supported by the local government. Therefore, the “Going
Abroad” strategy by local SOEs is aimed at meeting the financial needs of
the local government (Hubbard, 2016; Li et al., 2014). Compared to
central SOEs, local SOEs are more market-oriented and possess higher
management autonomy. The OFDI of private firms is also based on costbenefit considerations. However, central SOEs play a different role in
China’s overseas investment. As the name suggests, central SOEs are
supervised by the central government. Central SOEs undertake the
crucial task of national industrial policies and development priorities.
Thus, the OFDI of central SOEs is aimed at achieving national policy
objectives. As China becomes the world’s largest oil importer world­
wide, the issue of energy security has risen as a primary concern for
China. In order to ensure national energy security, central SOEs are
required to undertake more investment in low-risk countries. In order to
eliminate irrelevant OFDI, only projects from central State-OwnedEnterprises are included in the sample. The estimation results are dis­
played in Table 7. It could be concluded from Table 7 that the findings
still hold when only considering investment projects from central SOEs.
